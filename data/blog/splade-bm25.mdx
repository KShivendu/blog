> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

[SPLADE](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3) beats BM25 by learning a weight for every term instead of computing one. It also #[expands](adds terms the document never contained, so a query for "retirement" can match a document that only says "401k") documents with terms they don't contain, which is the part everyone talks about.

I wanted the opposite of the usual write-up. Not "look how much better the learned model is", but **which piece of BM25 the learned model should have kept**. It turns out to be the oldest piece: score every term both ways, keep whichever weight is higher, and SPLADE gains **+0.0201 NDCG@10** and wins on 12 of 13 datasets. One line, and it needs nothing SPLADE doesn't already have.

## SPLADE forgets words that are right there

Here is the whole problem in one query.

```
NanoFiQA2018 · "What approaches are there for pricing a small business?"
SPLADE scores 0.000 on this query. BM25 scores 0.289.

           word    w_query    w_doc    product
        pricing      2.164    2.189      4.738
       business      1.454    2.236      3.252
          small      2.199    0.000      0.000   <-- killed
```

Read the product column, since that is what actually scores. The gold document contains the word `small`. The query encoder weights it at **2.199**, one of the highest weights in the whole query. The document encoder gives it **zero**. The two sides are encoded independently and then multiplied, so either one zeroing a term kills the match, and a query BM25 partly answers comes back empty.

I checked whether that was a one-off and it isn't. Across the queries where SPLADE loses to BM25, the document encoder zeroes a content word that is present in the gold document **12.0%** of the time, against **4.5%** on the queries SPLADE wins. The query encoder is clean either way at about 3%, so the fault is on one side only. Other words it threw away with the query shouting about them: `macro` (query weight 2.629), `arte` (2.445), `strata` (2.137).

BM25 would have scored every one of them. It has no way _not_ to.

## The rule

For every term a document actually contains, score it both ways and keep whichever is higher.

```napkin
bm25(t,d) = idf(t) × tf(t,d)·(k1+1) / (tf(t,d) + k1·(1 - b + b·|d|/avgdl))
w[t]      = max( w_splade[t] , C · bm25(t,d) )        C = 0.31, k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75
```

Two details fall out of that. SPLADE's expansions survive untouched, because BM25 gives a term the document doesn't contain a score of zero, so the `max` never fires there. And a term SPLADE pruned to nothing gets re-added at its BM25 value, so we get two repairs out of one line: it **lifts** underweighted terms and **restores** deleted ones.

Applying it only to terms some query can reach gives bit-identical scores and adds far fewer postings, since a term no query touches can never change a ranking.

Observations:

- **The rule is worth +0.0201 NDCG@10**, 0.6253 to 0.6454, with a bootstrap CI of [+0.0125, +0.0281] and wins on **12 of the 13 datasets**. The only loss is NanoMSMARCO at −0.0091. Biggest gains are NanoSciFact +0.0540 and NanoFiQA2018 +0.0433.
- **The tail moves too**, which is what I care about most after [the mean hides where a change helps](/blog/relevance-tail). The share of queries returning #[nothing relevant](no judged-relevant document anywhere in the top 10, so NDCG@10 is exactly 0) drops from 12.2% to 10.3%, and p25 goes 0.351 to 0.360.
- **It's mostly a reordering fix, not a retrieval fix.** Recall@10 gains +0.0160 while recall@100 gains only +0.0057, so the rule is pulling relevant documents from rank 30 or 60 up into the top 10 rather than finding ones SPLADE missed entirely.
- **A query-side floor buys tail and nothing else.** Adding `max(w_q, 0.25·idf)` on the query vector moves the mean by +0.0009 (noise) but p25 by +0.019 and recall@10 by +0.0038. Worth having if the weak-query quartile is what you're optimising, and it costs a win on the per-dataset record (11/2 instead of 12/1).

### Lift and restore are separate repairs, roughly additive

Split the rule into its two halves and we can see it repairing two different failures:

| variant                                | NDCG@10    | vs SPLADE   | W/L  |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ---- |
| lift only, never re-add a pruned term  | 0.6379     | +0.0126     | 12/1 |
| re-add only, never touch a kept weight | 0.6325     | +0.0071     | 8/5  |
| both, the full rule                    | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | 12/1 |

Lift is the bigger half at about 1.8x, and the two are close to additive. So SPLADE underweights terms it keeps _and_ deletes terms it should keep, at different rates, and both are worth repairing.

Taking the higher of the two scores also beats adding them. With the same query-side floor on both sides of the comparison, the additive version gets **+0.0130** where the `max` gets **+0.0195**, because summing double-counts every term the two systems already agree on, while `max` only intervenes where they disagree.

## How simple can the BM25 be?

The rule's job is to be a backstop, so I tried removing pieces of BM25 from it. Each form gets its own sweep of `C`, since dropping `tf` moves the scale by an order of magnitude.

| form inside the floor                   | NDCG@10    | vs SPLADE   | W/L  |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ---- |
| `idf · tf(k1+1)/(tf + k1·L)`, full BM25 | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | 12/1 |
| drop length normalisation (b=0)         | 0.6428     | +0.0174     | 10/3 |
| `idf` alone, no tf at all               | 0.6425     | +0.0171     | 10/3 |
| `idf · sqrt(tf)`                        | 0.6406     | +0.0153     | 12/1 |
| `idf · (1 + ln tf)`                     | 0.6385     | +0.0131     | 10/3 |
| `idf · tf`, raw tf-idf                  | 0.6280     | +0.0027     | 8/5  |
| `idf · tf / L`, no saturation           | 0.6252     | −0.0001     | 8/5  |

Observations:

- **`max(w_splade, C·idf(t))` keeps 85% of the gain**, +0.0171 of +0.0201, with no term frequencies, no document lengths, no `avgdl` to maintain and one constant. For most people that's the version to implement.
- **The two ways of removing saturation both fail.** `idf·tf/L` is the k1 → ∞ limit and is worth nothing. Raw tf-idf is worse, −0.0546 once `C` is pushed to 0.2.
- **But `idf` alone is the k1 → 0 limit, and that works.** Read those two rows together and we get something odd about BM25: its tf machinery is doing damage control here rather than adding signal. The whole value of `k1` is stopping `tf` from mattering, and taking that to its limit costs 0.003.

That last point squares with SPLADE's own behaviour. Its weight for a term grows only 1.19x from tf=1 to tf=2, against BM25's 1.30x at k1=0.9, and above tf=10 it _falls_, since SPLADE #[max-pools](it keeps the highest weight any single position gave a term, and a max cannot grow with repeats) over positions instead of summing them.

Subword pieces turn out to be unremarkable. They match content words at every percentile on all three quantities, which is why restricting the rule to content-plus-subword never beat flooring everything. The overrides look like ordinary morphology: `##ise` 0.018 → 1.235, `##ers` 0.451 → 1.214, `##isation` 0.531 → 1.109.

Note that subword _literals_ have the highest SPLADE weights of any type at p50 1.408, which made me expect the opposite. But that measures terms present in SPLADE's vector, and the rule acts on terms present in the document, which includes the 28.8% SPLADE pruned to zero. Include the zeros and subword p50 drops to 0.928, right next to content.

Stopwords are the interesting row. SPLADE deletes most query-reachable stopwords outright and the rule puts every one of them back, so they end up as 35% of all the terms it touches and 12.4% of the final weight mass. Excluding stopwords and punctuation costs **+0.0190 instead of +0.0201**, both at 12 wins and 1 loss, which is inside the noise. So that 13% of added mass is free to drop.

One more thing falls out of that table. The rule ends up separating content words from stopwords by about 4.8x in final weight (1.232 against 0.257) where SPLADE's own literal weighting separates them by 2.3x (1.206 against 0.518). It is more discriminating about term type than SPLADE is, while containing no notion of term type at all, because IDF does that work implicitly.

## The cheap version

The setup someone would actually deploy is [inference-free](/blog/if-splade): encode documents once with the doc-only model and send raw query terms at weight 1.0, so there is no query-time model call. [`splade-v3-doc`](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3-doc) plus a BM25 floor:

| system                                  | NDCG@10    | vs base     | W/L  | recall@10 |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ---- | --------- |
| `splade-v3-doc`, inference-free queries | 0.5953     | -           | -    | 0.6281    |
| + floor at C=0.20                       | **0.6074** | **+0.0120** | 10/3 | +0.0230   |
| + floor at C=0.31                       | 0.5917     | −0.0036     | 7/6  | +0.0074   |

Real, with a CI of [+0.0013, +0.0236], but about half the symmetric model's gain, and the best `C` drops from 0.31 to 0.20. I expected the opposite, since this model's literal weights are much weaker to begin with (p50 0.641 against the symmetric model's 1.179) so there should be more to replace. The mechanism runs the other way: inference-free queries carry weight 1.0 on every term, so the query side contributes no discrimination at all and the document side carries the whole ranking signal. Flooring the document side therefore overwrites a larger share of the total, saturates sooner, and over-floors earlier.

## Why IDF wins a fight it should lose

This is the part I didn't expect. BM25's IDF is a corpus statistic that knows nothing about the sentence a word appears in. SPLADE's weights are learned end to end on the retrieval task with a transformer reading the whole passage. IDF should not be able to beat that on the document's own words, and it does.

Two numbers explain it. SPLADE deletes terms, aggressively, because it is trained with a [FLOPS regulariser](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05665) that penalises how many terms get a nonzero weight, and query-time cost is what that penalty is protecting. Deletion is usually right, since most of what it deletes is `the` and `for`. But it has no floor, so when it is wrong about a content word the term contributes exactly nothing instead of contributing a little. IDF cannot make that mistake, because a rare word gets a high score whether or not the model noticed it.

And the two are not really competing, which is what changed my mind about what this rule is. The `max` fires on 53% of content terms, so about half the time SPLADE's learned weight is already the higher of the two and survives untouched. The rule is not replacing a learned channel with a computed one. It is putting a floor under it.

<details>
<summary>One trap worth knowing if you try to fit SPLADE's weights yourself</summary>

I fitted BM25's IDF exponent against SPLADE's document weights and got **0.508**, which looks like a clean result saying BM25's IDF is twice as steep as it should be. Then I swapped in `sqrt(idf)` and it lost on 9 of 13 datasets.

The catch is that SPLADE's score is a _product_ of two vectors, so we have to fit the product and not a factor of it. The query side fits at 0.567, the document side at 0.508, and the exponent fitted directly on `w_q · w_d` is **0.9936**. BM25 applies IDF once at exponent 1.0. SPLADE splits the same total into two square roots, one per vector, and fitting one factor tells you nothing about the product.

</details>

## Key Takeaways

- Take the higher of SPLADE's learned weight and `C · bm25(t,d)` for every term a document contains, and NDCG@10 goes from 0.6253 to 0.6454 across 13 NanoBEIR datasets, winning on 12 of them, with the zero-result rate dropping from 12.2% to 10.3%.
- The rule survives dropping tf, k1, b and document lengths. `max(w_splade, 0.31·idf(t))` keeps 85% of the gain with one constant and no index statistics beyond document frequency.
- It repairs two separate failures at once. SPLADE underweights terms it keeps (+0.0126) and deletes terms it should have kept (+0.0071), and the two are close to additive.
- Removing tf saturation entirely fails, but ignoring tf entirely works. BM25's `k1` is damage control here rather than signal, which matches SPLADE's own weights barely tracking tf and reversing above tf=10.
- On the inference-free setup that people actually deploy, the same floor is worth +0.0120 with the constant retuned to 0.20.
