> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

[SPLADE](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3) beats BM25 by learning a weight for every term instead of computing one. It also #[expands](adds terms the document never contained, so a query for "retirement" can match a document that only says "401k") documents with terms they don't contain. That expansion is the part everyone talks about.

I wanted the opposite of the usual write-up. Not "look how much better the learned model is", but **which piece of BM25 the learned model should have kept**. It turns out to be the oldest piece. Score every term both ways, keep whichever weight is higher, and SPLADE gains **+0.0201 NDCG@10** on 12 of 13 datasets. One line, and it needs nothing SPLADE doesn't already have.

## SPLADE forgets words that are right there

Here is the whole problem in one query.

```
NanoFiQA2018 · "What approaches are there for pricing a small business?"
SPLADE scores 0.000 on this query. BM25 scores 0.289.

           word    w_query    w_doc    product
        pricing      2.164    2.189      4.738
       business      1.454    2.236      3.252
          small      2.199    0.000      0.000   <-- killed
```

Read the product column, that's what actually scores. The gold document contains the word `small`. The query encoder weights it **2.199**, near the top of the query. The document encoder gives it **zero**. The two sides are encoded separately and multiplied, so either one zeroing a term kills the match.

I checked whether that was a one-off. It isn't. On the queries where SPLADE loses to BM25, the document encoder zeroes a content word that sits in the gold document **12.0%** of the time. On the queries it wins, **4.5%**. The query encoder is clean either way at about 3%, so the fault is on one side only.

More words it threw away while the query was shouting about them: `macro` (query weight 2.629), `arte` (2.445), `strata` (2.137). BM25 would have scored every one. It has no way _not_ to.

## The rule

For every term a document contains, score it both ways and keep the higher one.

```napkin
bm25(t,d) = idf(t) × tf(t,d)·(k1+1) / (tf(t,d) + k1·(1 - b + b·|d|/avgdl))
w[t]      = max( w_splade[t] , C · bm25(t,d) )        C = 0.31, k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75
```

Two things fall out of that. SPLADE's expansions survive untouched, since BM25 scores a term the document lacks at zero and the `max` never fires. And a term SPLADE pruned to nothing comes back at its BM25 value. So one line does two repairs: it **lifts** underweighted terms and **restores** deleted ones.

Apply it only to terms some query can reach. The scores come out bit-identical and the index grows far less, because a term no query touches can never change a ranking.

Observations:

- **+0.0201 NDCG@10**, 0.6253 to 0.6454, bootstrap CI [+0.0125, +0.0281], winning on **12 of 13 datasets**. Best are NanoSciFact +0.0540 and NanoFiQA2018 +0.0433. The one loss is NanoMSMARCO at −0.0091.
- **The tail moves too**, which is what I care about most after [the mean hides where a change helps](/blog/relevance-tail). Queries returning #[nothing relevant](no judged-relevant document anywhere in the top 10, so NDCG@10 is exactly 0) drop from 12.2% to 10.3%, and p25 goes 0.351 to 0.360.
- **It reorders, it doesn't retrieve.** Recall@10 gains +0.0160 but recall@100 only +0.0057. So the rule pulls relevant documents from rank 30 or 60 into the top 10. It isn't finding ones SPLADE missed.
- **The same floor on the query vector buys tail only.** `max(w_q, 0.25·idf)` moves p25 by +0.019 and the mean by nothing. Take it if the weak-query quartile is what you're optimising.

### Lift and restore are separate repairs

| variant                                | mean       | vs SPLADE   | p50       | p25   | zero  | W/L  |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | --------- | ----- | ----- | ---- |
| SPLADE v3                              | 0.6253     | -           | 0.631     | 0.351 | 12.2% | -    |
| lift only, never re-add a pruned term  | 0.6379     | +0.0126     | 0.649     | 0.356 | 11.4% | 12/1 |
| re-add only, never touch a kept weight | 0.6325     | +0.0071     | 0.631     | 0.346 | 10.8% | 8/5  |
| both, the full rule                    | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | **0.672** | 0.360 | 10.3% | 12/1 |

Lift is the bigger half, about 1.8x. The two are close to additive. So SPLADE underweights terms it keeps _and_ deletes terms it should keep, and both are worth fixing.

Keeping the higher score also beats adding the two, **+0.0195** against **+0.0130**. Summing double-counts every term the two already agree on. The `max` only steps in where they disagree.

## How simple can the BM25 be?

The rule is a backstop, so I tried removing pieces of BM25 from it. Each form gets its own sweep of `C`, since dropping `tf` moves the scale by an order of magnitude.

| form inside the floor                   | mean       | vs SPLADE   | p50       | p25       | zero  | W/L  |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | --------- | --------- | ----- | ---- |
| SPLADE v3, no floor                     | 0.6253     | -           | 0.631     | 0.351     | 12.2% | -    |
| `idf · tf(k1+1)/(tf + k1·L)`, full BM25 | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | 0.672     | 0.360     | 10.3% | 12/1 |
| drop length normalisation (b=0)         | 0.6428     | +0.0174     | **0.682** | 0.379     | 10.6% | 10/3 |
| `idf` alone, no tf at all               | 0.6425     | +0.0171     | 0.673     | 0.372     | 10.5% | 10/3 |
| `idf · sqrt(tf)`                        | 0.6406     | +0.0153     | 0.651     | **0.380** | 10.6% | 12/1 |
| `idf · (1 + ln tf)`                     | 0.6385     | +0.0131     | 0.647     | **0.380** | 10.9% | 10/3 |
| `idf · tf`, raw tf-idf                  | 0.6280     | +0.0027     | 0.631     | 0.339     | 11.9% | 8/5  |
| `idf · tf / L`, no saturation           | 0.6252     | −0.0001     | 0.631     | 0.342     | 11.9% | 8/5  |

Observations:

- **`max(w_splade, C·idf(t))` keeps 85% of the gain**, +0.0171 of +0.0201. No term frequencies, no document lengths, no `avgdl` to maintain, one constant. For most people that's the version to implement.
- **Both ways of removing saturation fail.** `idf·tf/L` is the k1 → ∞ limit and is worth nothing. Raw tf-idf is worse, −0.0546 once `C` reaches 0.2.
- **But `idf` alone is the k1 → 0 limit, and it works.** Read those two rows together and BM25's tf term looks like damage control rather than signal. All `k1` does here is stop `tf` from mattering, and taking that to its limit costs 0.003.
- **The mean and the tail want different forms.** Full BM25 wins the mean at 0.6454, but its p25 of 0.360 is the _worst_ of the four working forms. Dropping length normalisation gives the best p50 at 0.682, and `sqrt(tf)` and `log(tf)` tie for the best p25 at 0.380, a full +0.020 over full BM25. If weak queries are what you care about, take the simpler form and lose a little mean.

SPLADE agrees. Its weight grows 1.19x from tf=1 to tf=2 where BM25 grows 1.30x, and above tf=10 it _falls_. It #[max-pools](it keeps the highest weight any single position gave a term, and a max cannot grow with repeats) over positions instead of summing them.

Subword pieces are unremarkable. They match content words at every percentile on all three quantities. That's why restricting the rule to content-plus-subword never beat flooring everything. The overrides are ordinary morphology: `##ise` 0.018 → 1.235, `##ers` 0.451 → 1.214, `##isation` 0.531 → 1.109.

Note that this holds only because the rule acts on terms present in the _document_. Count only terms present in SPLADE's vector and subwords look like the highest-weighted type of all, p50 1.408, because that hides the 28.8% pruned to zero.

Stopwords are the interesting row. SPLADE deletes most query-reachable stopwords and the rule puts every one back. They end up 35% of the terms it touches and 12.4% of the final weight mass. Dropping stopwords and punctuation costs **+0.0190 instead of +0.0201**, both at 12 wins and 1 loss. That 13% of added mass is free.

The rule also separates content words from stopwords by 4.8x in final weight, 1.232 against 0.257. SPLADE's own literal weighting separates them by 2.3x, 1.206 against 0.518. So the rule is more discriminating about term type than SPLADE is, while knowing nothing about term type. IDF does that work implicitly.

## The cheap version

The setup people actually deploy is [inference-free](/blog/if-splade). Encode documents once with the doc-only model, send raw query terms at weight 1.0, no query-time model call. [`splade-v3-doc`](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3-doc) plus a BM25 floor:

| system                                  | mean       | vs base     | p50   | p25   | zero  | W/L  |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ---- |
| `splade-v3-doc`, inference-free queries | 0.5953     | -           | 0.631 | 0.303 | 14.3% | -    |
| + floor at C=0.20                       | **0.6074** | **+0.0120** | 0.631 | 0.316 | 12.5% | 10/3 |
| + floor at C=0.31                       | 0.5917     | −0.0036     | 0.631 | 0.315 | 14.5% | 7/6  |

Real, CI [+0.0013, +0.0236], but about half the symmetric model's gain. The best `C` also drops from 0.31 to 0.20. Inference-free queries weight every term 1.0, so the query side does no discriminating and the document side carries the whole ranking. Flooring it therefore overwrites more of the total, and over-floors sooner.

## Why IDF wins a fight it should lose

This is the part I didn't expect. BM25's IDF is a corpus statistic. It knows nothing about the sentence a word sits in. SPLADE's weights are learned end to end on the retrieval task, by a transformer that read the whole passage. IDF should not beat that on the document's own words.

Two numbers explain it. SPLADE deletes terms aggressively, because it trains with a [FLOPS regulariser](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05665) that penalises how many terms get a nonzero weight. Deletion is usually right, since most of what it deletes is `the` and `for`. But there's no floor under it. When it's wrong about a content word, that term contributes exactly nothing instead of a little. IDF can't make that mistake, because a rare word scores high whether or not the model noticed it.

And the two aren't really competing. The `max` fires on 53% of content terms, so about half the time SPLADE's weight is already the higher one and survives untouched. This is a floor under a learned channel, not a replacement for it.

One note if you go looking for the exponent yourself. SPLADE's document weights fit `idf^0.508` and its query weights fit `idf^0.567`, but the product `w_q · w_d` fits **0.9936**. BM25 applies IDF once at exponent 1.0. SPLADE splits the same total into two square roots, so fitting either side alone says BM25 is twice too steep when it isn't.

## Key Takeaways

- Take the higher of SPLADE's learned weight and `C · bm25(t,d)` for every term a document contains. NDCG@10 goes 0.6253 to 0.6454 over 13 NanoBEIR datasets, winning on 12, and the zero-result rate drops from 12.2% to 10.3%.
- The rule survives dropping tf, k1, b and document lengths. `max(w_splade, 0.31·idf(t))` keeps 85% of the gain with one constant and no index statistics beyond document frequency.
- It fixes two separate failures. SPLADE underweights terms it keeps (+0.0126) and deletes terms it should have kept (+0.0071), and the two are close to additive.
- On the inference-free setup people actually deploy, the same floor is worth +0.0120 with the constant retuned to 0.20.
