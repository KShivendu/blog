> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

[SPLADE](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3) learns a weight for every word instead of computing one. It also #[expands](adds terms the document never contained, so a query for "retirement" can match a document that only says "401k") each document with words it never had. Those added words get all the attention.

I went looking at the other half, the words the document already has. SPLADE gets some of those badly wrong. Sometimes it scores a word at zero while the word is sitting right there in the text. BM25 never does that, so I used BM25 as a floor: score every word both ways and keep the higher one. NDCG@10 goes from **0.6253 to 0.6454** across 13 NanoBEIR datasets, and the share of queries that come back with nothing useful drops from **12.2% to 10.3%**. It wins on 12 of the 13.

## SPLADE forgets words that are right there

One query shows the whole thing.

```
  query 106                        the document it should find (id 76695)

  "What approaches are there       "...this is my academic understanding of
   for pricing a small              business pricing... from my research, it
   business?"                       looks like many small business use Seller
                                    Discretionary Earnings (SDE)..."

  SPLADE encodes the two sides separately, then multiplies them term by term:

  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   term       in doc    w_query      w_doc     product
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   pricing        x1      2.164  ×   2.189  =    4.738
   business       x7      1.454  ×   2.236  =    3.252
   small          x1      2.199  ×   0.000  =    0.000   ← the match dies
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  SPLADE ranks this document nowhere       NDCG@10   0.000
  BM25 finds it                            NDCG@10   0.289
```

The product column is what scores. `small` is in the document, and the query side weights it **2.199**, near the top of the query. The document side gives it **zero**. SPLADE encodes the two sides separately and multiplies them, so a zero on either side kills the match.

That's not a one-off. On the queries where SPLADE loses to BM25, the document side zeroes a content word that's in the right document **12.0%** of the time. On the queries it wins, **4.5%**. The query side is clean either way at about 3%, so only one side is at fault.

Other words it dropped while the query was shouting about them: `macro` (query weight 2.629), `arte` (2.445), `strata` (2.137). BM25 would have scored all of them. It has no way _not_ to.

## The rule

For every term a document contains, score it both ways and keep the higher one.

$$
\mathrm{bm25}(t,d) = \mathrm{idf}(t)\cdot\frac{f_{t,d}\,(k_1+1)}{f_{t,d} + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\frac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\right)}
$$

$$
w_t = \max\big(\,w^{\text{splade}}_t\;,\; C\cdot \mathrm{bm25}(t,d)\,\big)
\qquad C = 0.31,\; k_1 = 1.2,\; b = 0.75
$$

SPLADE's added words are safe. BM25 scores a word the document doesn't have at zero, so the `max` never fires on them. And a word SPLADE zeroed comes back at its BM25 value. One line, two fixes: it **lifts** words SPLADE underrated and **puts back** words it deleted.

Only bother with words some query asks for. The scores come out identical and the index grows much less, since a word no query touches can never change a ranking.

Observations:

- **+0.0201 NDCG@10**, 0.6253 to 0.6454, bootstrap CI [+0.0125, +0.0281], winning on **12 of 13 datasets**. Best are NanoSciFact +0.0540 and NanoFiQA2018 +0.0433. The one loss is NanoMSMARCO at −0.0091.
- **The tail moves too**, which is what I care about most after [the mean hides where a change helps](/blog/relevance-tail). Queries returning #[nothing relevant](no judged-relevant document anywhere in the top 10, so NDCG@10 is exactly 0) drop from 12.2% to 10.3%, and p25 goes 0.351 to 0.360.
- **It reorders, it doesn't retrieve.** Recall@10 gains +0.0160 but recall@100 only +0.0057. So the rule pulls relevant documents from rank 30 or 60 into the top 10. It isn't finding ones SPLADE missed.
- **The same floor on the query vector buys tail only.** `max(w_q, 0.25·idf)` moves p25 by +0.019 and the mean by nothing. Take it if the weak-query quartile is what you're optimising.

### Lift and restore are separate repairs

| variant                                | mean       | vs SPLADE   | p50       | p25   | zero  | W/L  |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | --------- | ----- | ----- | ---- |
| SPLADE v3                              | 0.6253     | -           | 0.631     | 0.351 | 12.2% | -    |
| lift only, never re-add a pruned term  | 0.6379     | +0.0126     | 0.649     | 0.356 | 11.4% | 12/1 |
| re-add only, never touch a kept weight | 0.6325     | +0.0071     | 0.631     | 0.346 | 10.8% | 8/5  |
| both, the full rule                    | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | **0.672** | 0.360 | 10.3% | 12/1 |

Lifting is worth about 1.8x more than putting back, and the two roughly add up. SPLADE makes both mistakes, underrating words it keeps and deleting words it shouldn't, and each is worth fixing separately.

Keeping the higher score also beats adding the two together, **+0.0195** against **+0.0130**. Adding them double-counts every word the two already agree on. The `max` only steps in where they disagree.

<summary>How much of BM25 do you actually need? (85% of it comes from `idf` alone)</summary>

Strip out the word counts and document lengths entirely, so the floor is just $0.31\cdot\mathrm{idf}(t)$, and you still get **0.6425 against the full rule's 0.6454**. That's 85% of the gain from one constant, with no average document length to maintain and no per-document normalisation. Worth taking if you don't want the bookkeeping.

| form inside the floor                   | mean       | vs SPLADE   | p25       | zero  | W/L  |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | --------- | ----- | ---- |
| `idf · tf(k1+1)/(tf + k1·L)`, full BM25 | **0.6454** | **+0.0201** | 0.360     | 10.3% | 12/1 |
| `idf` alone, no word counts at all      | 0.6425     | +0.0171     | 0.372     | 10.5% | 10/3 |
| `idf · sqrt(tf)`                        | 0.6406     | +0.0153     | **0.380** | 10.6% | 12/1 |
| `idf · tf`, raw tf-idf, no saturation   | 0.6280     | +0.0027     | 0.339     | 11.9% | 8/5  |

The one thing not to do is drop the saturation and keep the raw word count. Plain $\mathrm{idf}\cdot f_{t,d}$ falls to 0.6280, and pushing its constant up makes it far worse, −0.0546 at C=0.2. Word counts either need BM25's saturation or they need leaving out.

Which fits SPLADE, whose own weight grows only 1.19x from one occurrence to two and _drops_ past ten. Note the tail column too: full BM25 wins the mean but has the worst p25 of the working forms, and `sqrt(tf)` gives up 0.005 on the mean for +0.020 on p25.

</details>

Subword pieces are unremarkable. They match content words at every percentile on all three quantities. That's why restricting the rule to content-plus-subword never beat flooring everything. The overrides are ordinary morphology: `##ise` 0.018 → 1.235, `##ers` 0.451 → 1.214, `##isation` 0.531 → 1.109.

Note that this holds only because the rule acts on terms present in the _document_. Count only terms present in SPLADE's vector and subwords look like the highest-weighted type of all, p50 1.408, because that hides the 28.8% pruned to zero.

Stopwords are the interesting row. SPLADE deletes most query-reachable stopwords and the rule puts every one back. They end up 35% of the terms it touches and 12.4% of the final weight mass. Dropping stopwords and punctuation costs **+0.0190 instead of +0.0201**, both at 12 wins and 1 loss. That 13% of added mass is free.

The rule also separates content words from stopwords by 4.8x in final weight, 1.232 against 0.257. SPLADE's own literal weighting separates them by 2.3x, 1.206 against 0.518. So the rule is more discriminating about term type than SPLADE is, while knowing nothing about term type. IDF does that work implicitly.

## The cheap version

The setup people actually deploy is [inference-free](/blog/if-splade). Encode documents once with the doc-only model, send raw query terms at weight 1.0, no query-time model call. [`splade-v3-doc`](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3-doc) plus a BM25 floor:

| system                                  | mean       | vs base     | p50   | p25   | zero  | W/L  |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ---- |
| `splade-v3-doc`, inference-free queries | 0.5953     | -           | 0.631 | 0.303 | 14.3% | -    |
| + floor at C=0.20                       | **0.6074** | **+0.0120** | 0.631 | 0.316 | 12.5% | 10/3 |
| + floor at C=0.31                       | 0.5917     | −0.0036     | 0.631 | 0.315 | 14.5% | 7/6  |

That holds up, CI [+0.0013, +0.0236], but it's about half what the full model gets, and the best `C` drops from 0.31 to 0.20. Inference-free queries give every word a weight of 1.0, so the query side does no sorting at all and the document side carries the whole ranking. The floor is overwriting a bigger share of the signal, so it goes too far sooner.

## Why IDF wins a fight it should lose

This is the part I didn't expect. BM25's IDF is a corpus statistic. It knows nothing about the sentence a word sits in. SPLADE's weights are learned end to end on the retrieval task, by a transformer that read the whole passage. IDF should not beat that on the document's own words.

Here's what's going on. SPLADE deletes words aggressively, because training penalises it for every word it keeps (the [FLOPS regulariser](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05665)). Deleting is usually right, since most of what it deletes is `the` and `for`. But nothing catches it when it's wrong, and then a real word contributes exactly nothing instead of a little. IDF can't make that mistake. A rare word scores high whether or not the model noticed it.

The two also aren't fighting each other much. The `max` fires on 53% of content words, so roughly half the time SPLADE's own weight is already higher and stays. It's a floor under the learned weights, holding them up from below.

One note if you go looking for the exponent yourself. SPLADE's document weights fit `idf^0.508` and its query weights fit `idf^0.567`, but the product `w_q · w_d` fits **0.9936**. BM25 applies IDF once at exponent 1.0. SPLADE splits the same total into two square roots, so fitting either side alone says BM25 is twice too steep when it isn't.

## Key Takeaways

- Take the higher of SPLADE's learned weight and `C · bm25(t,d)` for every term a document contains. NDCG@10 goes 0.6253 to 0.6454 over 13 NanoBEIR datasets, winning on 12, and the zero-result rate drops from 12.2% to 10.3%.
- The rule survives dropping tf, k1, b and document lengths. `max(w_splade, 0.31·idf(t))` keeps 85% of the gain with one constant and no index statistics beyond document frequency.
- It fixes two separate failures. SPLADE underweights terms it keeps (+0.0126) and deletes terms it should have kept (+0.0071), and the two are close to additive.
- On the inference-free setup people actually deploy, the same floor is worth +0.0120 with the constant retuned to 0.20.
