> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

Open OpenAI's o200k vocabulary and you find `the` **nine** times:

```
'the'   ' the'   '.the'   '(the'   ',the'   '-the'   '/the'   '_the'   '\tthe'
```

`get` is in there seventeen times, `value` sixteen. BPE glues whatever comes before a word onto its front, so a common word gets a slot for every character that can sit in front of it.

The root of it is the space. Even a tokenizer that glues nothing but the space holds every common word **twice**, `'the'` and `' the'`. Train BPE that way on 20 MB of Python with 65,536 slots and **almost half the vocabulary is duplicates**. 15,506 strings are stored twice, once with a space and once without, which uses up 31,012 slots.

Every shipped vocabulary I checked does this, and these duplicates are most of what they write. **Four out of five tokens** they emit on 20 MB of English web text (C4) are a word they also store in another form:

```

cl100k        GPT-3.5, GPT-4               79.7%
o200k         GPT-4o onwards               80.8%
Qwen3         Qwen                         78.4%
DeepSeek V2   DeepSeek                     81.1%
SmolLM2       HuggingFace                  78.0%
Gemma 2       Google                       87.1%
```

So why are we storing the space at all, let alone twice?

## Mark the absence, not the presence

ISBPE (implied-space BPE) never stores a space. The decoder puts one between every two tokens, and a `#` marks where it's missing:

```
'the cat sat'
  BPE    the | ' cat' | ' sat'                 space stored in the token
  ISBPE  the | cat | sat                       space implied

'a, b) c: d'
  BPE    a | , | ' b' | ) | ' c' | : | ' d'
  ISBPE  a | #, | b | #) | c | #: | d          # = no space here

'tokenizations'
  BPE    token | izations
  ISBPE  token | #izations
```

A `#` on the left of a token means "no space before me", and on the right "no space after me". When a word follows punctuation, the mark goes on the punctuation, so the word keeps the same slot wherever it appears:

```
'get_attraction'
  BPE    get | _ | att | raction       ' attraction' is a slot, 'attraction' isn't
  ISBPE  get | #_# | attraction        same 'attraction' as after a space
```

Try a few more:

<FlagSide />

`split` is now the same slot it uses in `' split'`. This rule is exact. A word chunk is a maximal run of letters, so whatever sits right before a word with no space is punctuation or a digit, and that token can carry the mark. On the code corpus that held **805,857 times out of 805,857**.

So a word never needs a `#` form. `format` used to be split across two slots, one used 1,433 times after a space and one used 5,740 times after punctuation. Now it's one slot used **7,162** times.

Observations:

- **On Python, 9.6% fewer tokens** (304,228 against 336,466). One line from the test file shows where they go:

  ```
  'self.accountemail:'
    BPE    self | . | ac | coun | te | mail | :     7 tokens
    ISBPE  self | #.# | account | #email | #:      5 tokens
  ```

  BPE learned `account` mostly as `' account'`, so after a dot it shatters.

- **The ids are more predictable too.** Compressed with `xz`, ISBPE's id stream is 4.9% smaller on code (1.3187 bits per byte of text against 1.3860). I use `xz` as a rough stand-in for how hard the stream is to model.
- **English gains under 1%** (0.7% fewer tokens). English puts a space before almost every word, so there's little to pool. Under a third of the English vocabulary is duplicates (30.0%), against almost half of the code one (47.3%).

**Why not make the space its own token?** That also gives each word one slot, but now every space costs a token. On the Python file that's 35% more tokens than BPE (454,187), and `xz` barely moves (1.3823 against 1.3860).

## Beating GPT-4's pretokenizer with glue entries

GPT-4's pretokenizer (cl100k) goes after the same waste from the other side. It lets a punctuation mark join the next word, so `lang.split` is `lang | .split`. That saves a token and costs a slot per form. It's how cl100k ends up with sixteen forms of `value`.

Retrained on my 20 MB, cl100k emits 283,269 tokens against ISBPE's 304,228. You can't stack the two directly, because ISBPE needs `.` to be its own token to carry the mark. So I add the joined tokens only where they pay. Train at 65,536 minus K slots, then give the K slots to the most frequent punctuation-word pairs. `split` keeps its one slot, and `.split` is an extra one. I call these **glue entries**.

<GlueSlider />

| 20 MB Python, 65,536 slots | tokens      | xz bits/byte | camelCase kept whole |
| -------------------------- | ----------- | ------------ | -------------------- |
| BPE                        | 336,466     | 1.3860       | 14.63%               |
| BPE, cl100k pretokenizer   | 283,269     | 1.3938       | 13.39%               |
| BPE, o200k pretokenizer    | 282,847     | 1.3921       | 0.00%                |
| ISBPE                      | 304,228     | **1.3187**   | **15.86%**           |
| ISBPE + 4,000 glue entries | **270,816** | 1.3342       | **15.86%**           |

Observations:

- **4,000 glue entries give 4.4% fewer tokens than cl100k** (270,816 against 283,269) and 4.3% fewer than o200k, with a smaller `xz` size than either. 500 entries already pass cl100k (283,078 tokens).
- **The first pairs are pure code.** `(self` comes first, seen 17,758 times in training, then `:param`, `=None`, `_name` and `.get`.
- **o200k splits every word at a capital letter**, so it keeps no camelCase identifier whole.
- **Glue entries aren't in the library yet.** They patch the vocabulary after training, and they're single level, so `.get` can't merge further into `.getattr`.

## A vocabulary 8x smaller

The freed slots can buy shorter sequences or a smaller vocabulary. Drag the size:

**With an eighth of the vocabulary, ISBPE still needs fewer tokens.** At 8,192 slots it emits 331,922 tokens on held-out Python, against BPE's 336,466 at 65,536. Seven of every eight rows of a model's embedding table are gone. English is different: ISBPE is level with BPE at 8,192 slots and only ahead from 16,384 up. ## Other scripts

<ScriptSpaces />

**Hindi.** GPT-2's text-splitting rule cuts a Hindi word at every vowel sign, because a vowel sign isn't a letter in Unicode. o200k and my pretokenizer count it as a letter. That alone cuts BPE's tokens by 63%, from 336,376 to 123,133. That gain belongs to the pretokenizer. ISBPE adds 0.9% on top, about the same as English.

**Chinese** has no spaces between words, so ISBPE has one extra rule: **between two Chinese or Japanese characters, no space is implied.** Chinese text then needs no marks at all:

```
'位于梅州市，于1997年开通。'   (located in Meizhou, opened in 1997)

BPE     位于 | 梅 | 州市 | ， | 于 | 1997 | 年开通 | 。
ISBPE   位于 | 梅 | 州市 | ， | 于 | #1997# | 年开通 | 。
```

ISBPE is level with BPE here, 0.15% more tokens (299,398 against 298,947). Four out of five of its token ends land on a word boundary, the same as BPE's (80.9% against 81.1%, with the [jieba](https://github.com/fxsjy/jieba) segmenter as the reference). o200k needs 4.7% fewer tokens than both, by joining punctuation to the next word, for example `，在`. I haven't tried glue entries on Chinese.

## It transfers to text it never saw

Train on Python, then read English it has never seen:

```
'This is a lot of what I do with the college students I see.'

  BPE            19 tokens    ' st' | udent | s
  ISBPE          16           students
```

A code corpus has `students` mostly glued to something, so BPE never learns `' students'`. ISBPE needs only one form.

| trained on | tested on    | ISBPE      | BPE        | ISBPE vs BPE |
| ---------- | ------------ | ---------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| code       | English (C4) | **2.067x** | 2.026x     | +2.0%        |
| English    | Python       | **1.695x** | 1.675x     | +1.2%        |
| English    | WikiText     | 2.390x     | **2.440x** | -2.1%        |

Compression ratio here is text bytes per byte of stored ids (frequent ids take 1 byte, the rest 2). WikiText is the one loss. It writes punctuation with spaces around it, so `,` shows up about 170 times as often as in C4 (23,262 against 136). The "no space" slots learned on C4 rarely fire there.

## What it costs

- **WikiText loses 2.1%**, as above.
- **Chinese is level, not better**, and o200k beats it by 4.7%.
- **Identifiers take slightly more pieces.** They average 2.42 tokens each on average against BPE's 2.37, although more are kept whole. `accountemail` drops from 4 pieces to 2, while `Segments` goes from 1 to 3 (`S | #eg | #ments`).
- **Small vocabularies lose** on English, from 4,096 slots down.

## Does it help an LLM?

I don't know yet. I didn't train one. A token saving doesn't carry over fully, because removing a token can make the rest harder to predict. A model that sees only the two tokens before keeps 7% of ISBPE's token saving on code, measured in bits per byte. `xz`, which reads the whole file, keeps 53%. The two even rank the placements differently. Only a trained model can settle it. [Goldman et al. (2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.134/) did train models and found compression tracks downstream quality, which is the stronger evidence. (Measured with GPT-2's text-splitting rule.)

## The pretokenizer

ISBPE's text-splitting rule is GPT-2's with three changes:

```
 ?[\p{L}\p{M}]++(?:['’](?i:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)(?!\p{L}))?   a word
 ?\p{N}++                                               a number
 ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]++                                     punctuation
\s++$|\s+(?!\S)|\s                                      whitespace
```

- **Combining marks count as letters** (`\p{M}`), from o200k. That's the Hindi fix.
- **A contraction stays on its word**, straight or curly apostrophe, from o200k. `don’t` is one chunk.
- **The contraction can't be followed by a letter** (`(?!\p{L})`). GPT-2's rule fires inside quoted strings and eats the first letter: `b'test'` becomes `b | 't | est | '`. That broke the "a word always follows punctuation" argument 206 times on code. With the guard it's zero.

## Using it

```python
from isbpe import ISBPE

tok = ISBPE().train(corpus_bytes, vocab_size=65536)
ids = tok.encode("self.fireEvent")
tok.decode(ids)                      # exact bytes back, always

tok.explain("self.fireEvent")
# self.fireEvent -> self | #.# | fire | #Event
```

There's a Python library and a Rust port that encodes about 19x faster and builds the same vocabulary. Every model here decodes all test files and 2,000 random strings back exactly. Existing models can't read ISBPE ids, so this needs a model trained on it. Where you control both ends, like a storage layer, you can use it today. Everything is one 20 MB corpus per language and one test file.

## Key Takeaways

- **Tokenizers store each common word many times.** o200k holds `the` nine times and `get` seventeen, and four out of five tokens they write are such a duplicate.
- **Marking the missing space instead removes the duplicates.** On Python: 9.6% fewer tokens than BPE trained the same way, and with an eighth of the vocabulary it still needs fewer. English and Hindi gain about 1%, and Chinese is level with one extra rule.
- **Add 4,000 glue entries and it beats GPT-4's pretokenizer** by 4.4% on tokens, with a smaller `xz` size.
