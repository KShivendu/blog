An AI agent that searches for you works in a loop. It searches, reads what came back, rewrites the query and searches again. Each read-and-rewrite is an LLM call, and each one takes seconds.

> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

The simplest LLM rewrite skips the reading. Search for "what does modulus mean" with BM25 and the right answer comes back at rank 10. Ask an LLM to write a short passage answering the query, add its words to the query, and the right answer jumps to rank 1. This is #[query2doc](Wang et al. 2023: an LLM writes a passage for the query, and its words are added to the query before searching), and across the 13 [NanoBEIR](https://huggingface.co/collections/zeta-alpha-ai/nanobeir-66e1a0af21dfd93e620cd9f6) datasets it lifts my BM25 from **57.41 to 62.36** #[nDCG@10](a 0 to 100 score for how high the relevant docs sit in the top 10). It also takes about 5 seconds per query, where BM25 takes a fraction of a millisecond.

Most of the words that help were already in the search results. A small model can read the results and pick them out. Here is the idea on one query, in six steps. Steps 4 and 5 are the method, **grounded expansion**:

Observations:

- **81% of the gain is words the query or the top 10 already had.** They give +4.03 of the +4.95.
- **The other words are a coin flip.** They add +1.00 on average, but help 67 queries and hurt 73.

Borrowing words from the top results is an old idea called **pseudo-relevance feedback**, and the hard part has always been which words. The top 10 docs hold about 500 words the query doesn't have (211 for modulus), and the LLM uses about 20. Adding the same number of words, the LLM's picks give **+3.65** and random picks −1.16. The classic method, #[RM3](it weights each top doc by its BM25 score and adds the most frequent words, so the top-ranked doc dominates), gives −0.37. For modulus its top doc is about self-awareness, and RM3's words drop the score to 0.

## Grounded expansion

Given a query and the docs BM25 found, a small model marks the words an LLM would write. Every word it adds comes from a returned doc, hence the name **grounded expansion**. The model is a #[cross-encoder](a model that reads the query and one doc together, so it judges each word in context): [`ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2`](https://huggingface.co/cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2) for the 6-layer version and [`ms-marco-MiniLM-L2-v2`](https://huggingface.co/cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L2-v2) for the 2-layer one.

Training it costs nothing new. Public LLM passages already exist for 20,000 MS MARCO training queries ([query2doc](https://huggingface.co/datasets/intfloat/query2doc_msmarco)). A word in a retrieved doc is a 1 if the passage uses it and a 0 if not. That trains in 4 minutes on one GPU, and the model never sees the test datasets.

What it learns is context. In step 4 of the animation, "navy" scores .89 in the naval-rank article, the relevant doc, and .04 in an article about an honorary title. A count of "navy" gives both docs the same number, and the best formula I could build from counts gains +0.90, against +2.96 for the model. Each word keeps its best score over the docs it reads, and the 20 best join the query, weighted by how sure the model is.

It still makes RM3's bet that the top docs are worth reading, so misleading docs mislead it too. It just chooses better. RM3 helps 149 queries and hurts 151. Grounded expansion helps 192 and hurts 99.

## Why not just rerank?

The model reads each top doc next to the query anyway, so why not let the same cross-encoder rerank them? I ran the MS MARCO cross-encoders as rerankers at both sizes, and also on top of grounded expansion. At the same size both cost the same per (query, doc) pair, 251 and 260 ms for 10 pairs with the 6-layer int8 models on my laptop. So the chart gives each system's cost as the pairs it reads.

- **At the same cost they tie.** At 5 pairs and 2 layers, expansion scores 59.46 and the reranker 59.31. At 10 pairs and 6 layers they score 60.37 and 60.68, and the reranker wins 167 queries and loses 180.
- **More pairs help the reranker, but it can only reorder.** On the top 20 it scores 61.48, still even per query with 6-layer expansion (188 to 185), and its recall@100 stays at 81.20. Expansion finds new docs and lifts recall@100 to 82.45.
- **They fail on different queries.** On ArguAna's whole-argument queries, like "A bargaining chip In much the same way...", expansion scores 0 and the reranker 100. On SciFact's "Arterioles have a larger lumen diameter than venules." the 6-layer reranker on the top 20 pushes the right doc from rank 1 out of the top 10.
- **Stacked, they come within 0.29 of the LLM.** Expansion then a reranker on its top 5 scores **62.07** against 62.36. Its p25 is 35.00 against 35.62, and its median of 63.72 is the best here. That is +1.70 over expansion alone, better on 125 queries and worse on 106.
- **I estimate it at about 130 ms, 40 times faster than the LLM.** The int8 expansion step takes 89 ms, and 5 reranker pairs add about half its model pass. I haven't timed it end to end.

## Can it loop and judge like an agent?

An agent reads the new results again and decides whether a rewrite helped. I tried both.

**Looping** the 2-layer version leaves the mean at 59.4 over three rounds, because round 2 helps 39 queries and hurts 29. It fixes "Commodore is only a land rank." (50 to 100) once round 1 lifts the relevant doc into the 5 docs the model reads. It can't fix "what does modulus mean", which drops from 28.9 to 0 when round 1 picks the physics sense ("measure, material, stress"). Round 2 then reads more physics.

**Judging** keeps a rewrite only if the 6-layer reranker rates the new top 5 higher than the old top 5. It costs 10 more pairs and needs no training.

- **It rescues RM3.** RM3 goes from 57.04 to 57.99 and beats always-on RM3 on 10 of 13 datasets, expanding 35% of queries.
- **It catches misleading results.** For "SummerSlam was a concert." BM25 has the right page, SummerSlam 2015, at rank 1. The 6-layer version adds "1999" and "1998" from the pages below it and drops the score to 38.7. The judge sees no gain and keeps BM25's 100, while it keeps Commodore's rewrite from 35.6 to 100.
- **It is too careful for grounded expansion.** It expands 40% of queries and scores 59.70, against 60.37 for always expanding. A perfect judge would reach 62.14, and the best one I trained gets 60.57.

## Key Takeaways

- **An LLM rewrite mostly adds words already in the top 10 docs.** It never sees them, yet those words carry 81% of its gain.
- **Grounded expansion does that reading with a small model.** It is relevance feedback that reads the docs instead of counting words. It trains on LLM passages that already exist and keeps +2.05 at 24 ms, where RM3's counting gives −0.37.
- **With a reranker on top it comes within 0.3 of the LLM.** 62.07 against 62.36, in an estimated 130 ms against 5.2 s.

<details>
<summary>Caveats and how the numbers were measured</summary>

- **Baseline.** BM25 (k1 1.2, b 0.75) on stemmed words with 33 stopwords removed, my [strongest lexical baseline](/blog/stemming-expansion). Query words count 5 times and added words count by the model's confidence: `q'(t) = 5 · qtf(t) + 2p(t)`.
- **The weight scale was chosen on the test set.** 2p was the best of 1, p and 2p, so up to about 0.5 of the gain may be that choice. At weight 1 the 6-layer version scores 59.81 (+2.40).
- **Part of the gain is in-domain.** The model trained on MS MARCO. Without the NanoMSMARCO queries it gains +1.80 at weight 1, against +3.64 for the LLM's own top-10 words.
- **It is stable.** Two seeds land 0.08 apart, 15.9k to 100k training queries land within 0.1, and swapping the LLM that wrote the training passages (text-davinci-003 or Claude Haiku) moves it 0.22.
- **Latency.** The models were timed alone on a dedicated 4-core CPU, one query at a time, and BM25 on my laptop. The LLM took 12,040 ms through the Claude CLI, about 6.8 s of it start-up, so its model call is an estimated 5.2 s.
- **Reranking, looping and judging.** The rerankers used int8 weights and 128 tokens per pair. The 6-layer expansion they are compared with reads 256-token docs at full precision, and at int8 and 128 tokens it scores 60.42. So the stacked result should hold at the estimated 130 ms, though I haven't run it that way. Looping cut docs to 128 words, so its round 1 scores 59.42 instead of 59.46. The trained judge is a logistic regression fitted on 12 datasets and tested on the 13th.
- **Reweighting the query's own words helps a little more.** The same model can mark which query words the answer needs. Doing both in one pass scores 61.13, level with the LLM's own top-10 words, so far only with the 6-layer version.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What did not work</summary>

- **Counting.** Picking each doc's highest-BM25-weight words finds the rarest word anywhere. On "Commodore is only a land rank." it picks "kapitan, cartridge, flotilla", where grounded expansion picks "guard, navy, admiral", and loses 5.96. Counts also can't tell which sense of the query a doc is about. For "how did the mini gun get its name" a counting formula picks "austin, morris" from the Mini car pages and falls from 100 to 50, where grounded expansion picks "rifle, bullet".
- **Reweighting the query words without reading the docs.** A model that sees only the query gains +0.51 in 1.8 ms.
