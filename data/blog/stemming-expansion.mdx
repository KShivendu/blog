> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

A search engine's `english` analyzer does a few things to your text before it hits the index: lowercase it, drop stopwords, and #[stem](strip suffixes so a word's inflections share one index term) so `race`, `racing` and `raced` all become one term, `race`. I wanted to know which of those actually matters, so I built a plain BM25 word index and tested each step one at a time over [13 NanoBEIR datasets](https://huggingface.co/collections/zeta-alpha-ai/nanobeir-66e1a0af21dfd93e620cd9f6).

I found the stemmer does most of the work. And if you think about it, you can do the stemmer's job on the query-expansion side instead of altering the index. Stemming folds `racing` into `race` in the index when you build it. Expansion leaves the index alone and rewrites the query `race` into `race OR racing OR raced` when you search. Same word family, just handled on a different side. Both land in nearly the same place.

Once you see the stemmer as query expansion, you can do even better by learning the expansions from the corpus, not Porter's fixed rules. That catches `high`/`higher`/`highest`, which no stemmer merges. If you are smart about weighting those learnt expansions, you can even beat Elasticsearch's `english` analyzer. In [Token Search](/blog/token-search) I run the same idea over BPE token IDs and drop the English pipeline entirely.

<StemExpandHero />

The top lane is stemming: at build time every form folds into one term, the query with it, and the index is rewritten. The bottom lane is expansion: the index stays as-is and the query fans into its family, weighted, best match wins. On `race` both reach the same four forms. Switch to `high` and Porter can't merge the family at all, but a learned expansion still reaches it.

## The stemmer makes the biggest jump

Start with a plain BM25 word index (lowercase, split on non-letters, no stemming, no stopwords), then add one step at a time. Numbers are NDCG@10 over the 645 NanoBEIR queries Elasticsearch's `english` analyzer returns results for, the same set every chart in this post uses, all at Lucene's default BM25 (k1=1.2, b=0.75), so the bars are always comparable. We show the median (p50) and the weak-query quartile (p25) next to the mean, since [the mean hides where a change actually helps](/blog/relevance-tail).

Three things in that chart matter:

- **Stemming is the whole lever.** It lifts the mean from **0.5522** to **0.5747**, a **+0.022** jump, and drags the weak-query tail up with it (p25 0.283 to 0.301).
- **Stopwords add a little.** On their own they add **+0.008** (0.5522 to 0.5606), and **+0.005** stacked on stemming (0.5747 to 0.5793 for the full `word_en` pipeline). Small next to stemming's +0.022, since BM25's IDF already down-weights `the`/`of`/`and` to almost nothing, but not quite nothing.

So the whole English pipeline comes down to the stemmer. And you can do its job from the query side instead.

## The same collapse, moved to the query

Index-side stemming rewrites everything. When the index is built, every word in every document is replaced by its stem, and so is every query word. `race`, `races`, `racing` and `raced` all become the single word `race` on both sides.

Query expansion does the same merge from the other end, using the same stemmer, just on the query instead of the index. Leave the index as the original words, untouched. At search time, run Porter on the query word `race`, pull every indexed word that stems to the same thing, and search for all of them at once. The stemmer that would have collapsed the documents now expands the query.

```
race  ->  race, races, racing, raced      (Porter stems all four to "race")
```

The documents are never touched, so the four forms stay as four separate words in the index. To match them from one query word, we have to make two choices stemming never faces: how much to trust each variant, and how to combine them.

**Trust: weight each variant by attestation.** A form that barely appears is a weaker signal than one that's everywhere, so each variant gets a weight from its own document frequency, `df_variant / (df_variant + 0.5 · df_query)`. That weight scales the variant's IDF. The full BM25 score then multiplies this by a term-frequency factor per document, `tf·(k1+1) / (tf + k1·(1 - b + b·dl/avgdl))`, which is 1 for a single hit in an average-length document and climbs with repeats. The table below is the IDF side, one plain hit's worth; the tf side is the mean lever and comes later. On NanoDBPedia (N=6045 documents), where `race` sits in 258:

| variant  | df  | weight | idf  | weight·idf | stem idf |
| -------- | --- | ------ | ---- | ---------- | -------- |
| `race`   | 258 | 1.00   | 3.15 | **3.15**   | 2.59     |
| `racing` | 230 | 0.64   | 3.27 | 2.09       | 2.59     |
| `races`  | 92  | 0.42   | 4.18 | 1.74       | 2.59     |
| `raced`  | 19  | 0.13   | 5.74 | **0.74**   | 2.59     |

`race` is the query word itself, so it's pinned to weight 1.00. Every other form runs through the formula, which climbs toward 1 as a variant gets more common but never reaches it (even `race` as a variant of a rarer query word tops out near 0.97), so no expansion ever outweighs the word you typed. Read the `weight·idf` column, not the raw IDF: `racing` is nearly as common as `race`, so it's trusted almost fully, while `raced` is rare and its hit is discounted hard, from a raw 5.74 down to **0.74**. The `stem idf` column is what index-side stemming assigns the same match instead: it flattens all four forms into one term at the union IDF, a single **2.59** for every form. Trusting every variant equally instead costs **0.010** on the mean (0.545 to 0.535), because the rare junk forms Porter drags in start scoring like the real word.

<details>
<summary>Doesn't a rare variant's high IDF undo its low weight?</summary>

It nearly does, and cancelling that is the whole point of the weight. Read the raw `idf` column in the table above alone and it's backwards: unweighted, `raced` contributes **5.74** to a document that holds it, above the actual query word `race` at **3.15**. BM25 would rank the rare inflection over the word you searched for, pure rarity windfall.

The weight cancels it. `raced`'s 0.13 pulls its 5.74 down to **0.74**, back under `race`'s 3.15, so the family lands in the right order: race > racing > races > raced. But 0.74 isn't nothing. The high IDF still cushions the low weight (0.13 · 5.74 = 0.74, not 0.13 · 3.15 = 0.41), so a rare variant still counts, it just can't outrank the word you typed.

Compare the `weight·idf` and `stem idf` columns in that table and you can see the flattening cut both ways. Stemming merges all four forms into one term at the union document frequency (454 documents), so every hit, `race` or `raced`, scores the same flattened **2.59**. It dilutes the query word (`race` drops from its own 3.15 to 2.59), which is exactly what costs stemming the weak-query tail. And it over-credits the rare form (`raced` jumps from 0.74 to 2.59), scoring a `raced`-only document as if it held the word you typed. Query-side keeps both sharp: the exact word at 3.15, the rare variant at 0.74.

</details>

**Combine: don't just add the variants up.** A document that matches several forms shouldn't score as if it matched several different words. Add each form's own score and a common form like `racing` piles its full weight onto every motorsport document, race or not, and the mean drops to **0.568**. Two things cap that: take the single best-matching form (`max`), or fold the forms into one term and score them under a single saturation, the way stemming does by folding the family into one word. The chart below runs both, and a couple of variations.

Run them across the 13 datasets, all on the Porter family and the same stopword-stripped base:

- **Expansion reproduces stemming.** Make the two choices stemming makes, score every form at the shared stem IDF and count the forms in a document as one, and query-side lands on the stemmed index to within 0.001 (**0.5795** mean, 0.6309 median, 0.3066 tail), matching it on all but a dozen or so queries. But merge the family the other way, one union IDF over the attestation-weighted term-frequencies with a single saturation, and you edge _past_ stemming to **0.5819**, the best combine here. Weighting which forms count, then aggregating them under one saturation, is the seed of the document-side trick later.

<details>
<summary>Why the missing piece is tf aggregation, not the IDF</summary>

The 0.002 mean gap (stemming over the best-match expansion) has an obvious suspect. Index-side stemming scores every form through one merged IDF, and expansion uses each variant's own IDF instead. But scoring the whole stem family through a single union IDF makes it worse, not better, because it dilutes the exact query word's own IDF.

Take `what album is help by the Beatles on`. Alone, `help` has IDF 3.78. Merge it with `helped`, `helping` and `helps` and the shared IDF drops to 3.33, so the word carrying the query loses its edge and the gold document falls from rank 1 to rank 3. Keeping `help` at its own 3.78 holds rank 1. The IDF isn't the mean gap.

The real gap is tf aggregation. I rebuilt index-side stemming from the separate posting lists: union IDF, sum the raw term-frequencies of every form in a document, one saturation. It lands right back on the stemmed index, within 0.001, matching it on all but a dozen or so queries. (Raw scores differ on the rest, always a short or numeric form the family drops like `25s` or `going`/`go`.)

A document that uses `race`, `racing` and `raced` gets one saturated count of all three under stemming, and only the single best form under expansion's `max`. That extra evidence is the mean edge. On one real NanoDBPedia document with `race` once and `racing` twice:

```
doc: race x1, racing x2  (length 44)   weights: race 1.0, racing 0.64
                                       idf: race 3.15, racing 3.27, union 2.59
  max        = max( 1.0·3.15·sat(1), 0.64·3.27·sat(2) ) = max(2.87, 2.70) = 2.87
  sum        = 2.59 · sat( 1.0·1 + 0.64·2 ) = 2.59 · sat(2.28) = 3.51
  stemming   = 2.59 · sat( 1 + 2 )          = 2.59 · sat(3)    = 3.87
```

`max` keeps `race` and throws the two `racing` hits away. Summing folds them in, so the merged count is 2.28, saturated once and scored at the union IDF (2.59, below `race`'s own 3.15). Stemming does the same with every form at full weight. Summing the term-frequencies is what reproduces the stemmed index. The `max` we use is the one real difference, and on this panel it barely moves the number.

</details>

So the two are the same idea, split only by where the merge happens.

A benchmark on my own BM25 is easy to fool, so I ran the same 13 datasets through Elasticsearch's `english` analyzer (Lucene BM25, standard tokenizer, possessive stripping, stopwords, a Porter stemmer), at its default `k1`=1.2. It gets **0.5786**. The query-side version, expanding each word into its Porter family with no index rewrite, gets **0.5774** at the same k1: a dead heat on NDCG, Elasticsearch ahead by a hair. Flip to recall and the query-side index pulls ahead, recall@10 **0.626 vs 0.620** and recall@100 0.817 vs 0.816, so it surfaces slightly more of the relevant documents and just orders the very top a touch differently.

## Past the stemmer

Porter is just suffix rules. It merges `race`, `races` and `racing` because it stems all three to `race`, and misses anything the rules don't cover. `high` stems to `high` but `higher` stems to `higher`, so they never meet, even though they're the same word. But the family is just a lookup, so we can build a better one from data.

Take every pair of words that look alike (same start, small change at the end). Keep a pair only if the two words also show up in the same kinds of sentences. That leaves a table of 27k words, each with a few learned partners. Stack it on top of Porter, so all of Porter's matches stay and the learned pairs add what its rules can't reach.

The similarity check is only a gate: keep a pair if the two words are close enough (a cutoff of 0.30). A learned variant then gets the same attestation weight as any Porter variant, `df_variant / (df_variant + 0.5 · df_query)`, combined by `max`. So the only change from the Porter run is which words are in the family, not how they score, and the +0.0033 comes from the new words alone.

<details>
<summary>How a learned pair gets its similarity and its weight, worked through</summary>

**Step 1, the similarity.** Take `dietary` and `diet`. For each word, we look at which words sit near it (within five words) across the corpus. Common neighbours like `the` get discounted, distinctive ones like `fiber` get boosted. That's PPMI (positive pointwise mutual information). Each word ends up as a list of weighted neighbours, and the similarity is the cosine between two such lists. Reproduced on the benchmark corpus, the shared neighbours and their PPMI weights look like this:

```
context word     PPMI(dietary)   PPMI(diet)   product
fiber                4.52           3.00        13.6
vegetarian           2.77           4.65        12.9
mediterranean        2.51           4.61        11.6
fat                  3.11           3.67        11.4
lifestyle            3.18           3.12         9.9
```

`dietary` and `diet` share 457 neighbours like these. Across them the dot product is 746 and the two norms are 32.8 and 29.9, so cosine = 746 / (32.8 · 29.9) = **0.76**, well over the 0.30 cutoff. Do the same for `dietary` and `algorithm` and they only share generic words like `baseline` and `evaluated`, so the cosine is **0.19**, under the cutoff and dropped.

This is exactly what suffix rules can't see. Porter stems `dietary` to `dietari` and `diet` to `diet`, so it keeps them apart, but they live in the same sentences.

**Step 2, the weight.** Passing the cutoff only adds `diet` to `dietary`'s family. Its weight is the usual attestation formula, from the document counts in the index we're searching. In NanoSciFact, `dietary` is in 51 documents and `diet` in 70:

```
diet     df 70   weight = 70 / (70 + 0.5 * 51) = 0.733
diets    df 15   weight = 15 / (15 + 0.5 * 51) = 0.370
dieting  df  1   weight =  1 / ( 1 + 0.5 * 51) = 0.038
```

So a search for `dietary` also searches `diet` at 0.73, `diets` at 0.37, and `dieting` at just 0.04, each combined by `max` against every document. A rare word lands near 0.04 and barely moves a score. That is what keeps a noisy pair table safe.

(The real pair table was built once on C4, a big general corpus, where `dietary`/`diet` scored 0.55. I reran the method on the benchmark corpus above just to show the numbers on familiar words.)

</details>

Where you set the gate matters at the top end. Toggle it above: drop to 0.25 and false friends like `cancer`/`canine` slip in, but they carry no real weight, so the mean holds at 0.5807. ## Where the weighting tops out

Porter plus learned pairs already edges Elasticsearch, so I spent a while trying to push the weighting itself further. It works. The best design keeps a query word at its own sharp IDF, not the diluted family IDF, whenever a document actually contains that word. Then it gates that boost by how much of the rest of the query the document matches. So a stray "van" in an unrelated document never earns the van-Gogh boost. Stack that weighting on the Porter-plus-learned family and it clears the analyzer on the exact same queries: **0.589 vs 0.579** on the mean, **0.333 vs 0.307** on the weak-query tail, and a lower zero-rate.

One catch. The gain is clean here, but the weighting is tuned on this 13-dataset panel, and the margin concentrates in morphological-question corpora like FiQA and Touché. Drop those two and it stops separating from plain stemming, so this is a same-panel win, not a proven-general one.

Here's why it can't go further. On entity queries, the ones where the weighting can't help, a learned sparse model like [SPLADE](/blog/splade-bm25) pulls far ahead. Reranking the same candidate pool, it beat this BM25 by **+0.22** NDCG on NanoMSMARCO and **+0.23** on NanoNQ. It wins by expanding `gogh` to `vincent` and `belle` to `beauty`, semantic links that tell van-Gogh-the-painter from a passing "van". My co-occurrence table holds none of those. It only has spelling neighbours like `belle` to `bell`, so no amount of lexical weighting reaches them.

That's the ceiling of this whole line of work. Stemming and learned morphology carry a plain word index all the way to a production analyzer. Everything past that is semantic, and semantics needs a trained model, not a longer word list.

<details>
<summary>Anchoring, coordination, and the stack that edges Elasticsearch</summary>

Here's the failure a stemmer hides. Search `deductible` and Porter merges the whole family (`deductible`, `deduction`, `deducted`) into one term. Its document frequency is now the union, so its IDF drops. `deductible` alone is worth IDF **3.90**, the merged family only **2.53**. A document that literally says "deductible" gets scored as if the word were common.

**Anchoring** fixes that: score a document at the exact query word's own IDF (3.90) if it contains that word, and at the family IDF (2.53) if it only has a variant. Same insight as the tail earlier, the exact word deserves its own sharpness, applied to every document instead of just the weak-query tail.

Anchoring helps morphological queries but hurts entity queries, because it also boosts a rare token like `van` in documents that have nothing else to do with the query. So gate it by **coordination**, how much of the rest of the query the document matches:

```
Query: "van gogh paintings"
  relevant:    "van gogh's paintings ..."   matches van + gogh + paintings  -> boost ON
  distractor:  "... a delivery van ..."       matches only "van"              -> boost OFF
```

The best version, the winning bar in the chart above, folds the learned pairs into the family too, so the same anchoring and coordination run over Porter's morphology plus the learned comparatives and spellings. It's a pooled-significant gain over plain stemming: the 95% bootstrap interval stays above zero, stable across seeds, the scoring audited clean. But it never clears a held-out test once you drop FiQA and Touché. So it's a same-panel win, not a general one.

</details>

## Expand the document instead

Everything so far expands the query at search time. There is a mirror image: expand the document at index time and leave the query as plain words. That is what [Inference-free SPLADE](/blog/if-splade) does with a neural model, the encoder writes the expansions into each document and the query is just raw tokens, no model call. The same flip works for our lexical families, with no model at all.

The question is how much of the weighting survives the move. Three of the four pieces do.

- **Attestation weights** bake into the added term's impact.
- **Learned pairs** are just more terms to write into the document.
- **Anchoring comes for free.** A document keeps its own words at their sharp IDF, and the expanded forms go in at the reduced family IDF. Whether a document holds the exact query word is a fact about the document, known at index time, so the conditional anchoring we built on the query side is precomputable per document.

The one piece that can't move is the **coordination gate**. It scales the anchor by how much of the rest of the query a document matches, and the rest of the query isn't known until search time. So it stays a query-side, rerank-stage step.

I ran the document-side version (attestation, learned pairs, anchoring, no coordination) against our query-side default across all 13 datasets.

- **Document-side anchoring beats our query-side `max`.** 0.5842 vs 0.5775 on the mean, and a big jump on the tail, 0.333 vs 0.301. A paired bootstrap puts the mean gap at **+0.007, 95% CI [+0.001, +0.013]**, above zero in 98.6% of resamples. It also clears index-side stemming, on the mean (0.5842 vs 0.5795) and the tail (0.333 vs 0.307).
- **It gets both halves at once.** The summed term-frequency aggregates evidence like stemming, which is the mean, and anchoring keeps the exact word at its own sharp IDF, which is the tail. `max` had the sharp IDF but discarded evidence. Stemming aggregated evidence but diluted the IDF. Document-side anchoring keeps both.
- **The query is a plain BM25 lookup again.** No family expansion and no posting-list merge at search time, and the scoring is additive, so WAND and BlockMax-WAND work. Coordination was the thing that broke those additive bounds and forced a rerank stage. Moving anchoring to the document keeps fast top-k and recovers most of coordination's gain without it.

The coordination gate is the one piece left on the query side, and it earns its keep in a specific place. Adding it back (coord-soft, 0.5894) buys another 0.005 over document-side anchoring, but the bootstrap can't separate them (96% of resamples, the interval crosses zero). Its contribution lands on the entity corpora: document-side anchoring's per-dataset losses are MSMARCO, ArguAna, and SCIDOCS, where anchoring over-boosts a lone-token match like a stray "van", and coordination is exactly what gates that. Where the families actually help, FiQA (+0.049), NQ (+0.021), Touché (+0.011), the document side already has it.

So the ES-level weighting is not stuck at query time. Bake it into the documents and the query stays a plain, fast, WAND-friendly BM25 lookup, the same trade [Inference-free SPLADE](/blog/if-splade) makes, minus the GPU.

## It only works in English

Every piece here is English machinery. Porter is English suffix rules, the stopword list is English, and the learned pairs were trained on English text, so they only know English words. Point this index at German or Chinese and none of it carries over. You would rebuild the stemmer, the stopword list, and the pair table once per language.

That per-language pipeline is the real cost of the query-side trick, not the accuracy. [Token Search](/blog/token-search) keeps the same machinery (expand a word into a family, weight the variants, take the `max`) but runs it over the tokenizer's own subword IDs instead of English words. The tokenizer is the only language-specific part, and one tokenizer already covers every language it was trained on. So the same method that matches Elasticsearch here needs no new stemmer, stopword list, or pair table for the next language. In that post it even beats a dedicated Chinese segmenter with zero setup.

## Key Takeaways

- Query-side Porter expansion over a plain word index matches Elasticsearch's `english` analyzer on NDCG (0.5774 vs 0.5786 across 13 NanoBEIR datasets, both at Lucene's default k1=1.2) and edges it on recall@10 (0.626 vs 0.620), with no destructive index rewrite.
- Stemming is the whole lever, **+0.022** over a plain index. Stopwords add a little, +0.008 alone and +0.005 stacked on top of stemming.
- A stemmer is one query expansion seen from the other side. Match its two choices, the shared stem IDF and the forms counted as one, and query-side reproduces it to within 0.001, matching it on all but a dozen or so queries. Keep each form's own IDF and take the best match instead, and you trade a little mean for a better weak-query tail.
- At Lucene's default k1, query-side expansion and Elasticsearch land in the same place, so the analyzer's machinery beyond stemming is decoration. Snowball expansion is slightly worse than Porter, and the possessive stripping and exact tokenizer don't move the needle.
- Once expansion makes the word family a swappable lookup, learned word pairs catch `high`/`higher`/`highest` and `behavioral`/`behaviour` that no suffix rule merges. All of it is English, so [Token Search](/blog/token-search) runs the same idea over BPE token IDs, config-free and multilingual.
- The weighting even moves to the document side. Bake attestation, learned pairs, and anchoring into each document and query with plain tokens, and it beats the query-side default (**+0.007**, bootstrap clears zero) while keeping search a fast, WAND-friendly BM25 lookup, the lexical take on [Inference-free SPLADE](/blog/if-splade).
