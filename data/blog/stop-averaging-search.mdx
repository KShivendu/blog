Nobody ships a service on _average_ latency. You report p50, p95, p99 — and "our average latency is 40ms" would get laughed out of the room, because everyone knows the average hides the tail that's actually hurting users. Yet we report search quality as a single average NDCG all the time, and somehow that's normal. Same mistake, and we've normalized it for quality.

Here's how bad it gets. I ran a plain lowercase-word BM25 baseline over 12 [NanoBEIR](https://huggingface.co/collections/zeta-alpha-ai-nanobeir) datasets (599 queries), and instead of collapsing each metric to a mean, I pooled the per-query scores and read off the percentiles. The mean NDCG@10 is **0.526** — a respectable-looking number. The **p10 is 0.000**. More than one in ten queries retrieves nothing relevant at all in the top 10 — total failures — and the average buries every one of them. You would never know one in ten searches returns garbage from looking at "0.53."

## One in ten searches returns nothing

The mean of a per-query metric is dominated by the easy queries and hides the tail of queries that fail — which, for search, is the part that matters. Pool the per-query scores and the distribution says something the mean never does:

Three things in that chart matter:

1. **NDCG@10 and recall@10 both floor at p10 = 0.000.** More than 10% of queries score a hard zero — nothing relevant in the top 10 — while the mean sits near 0.53 for both. The average doesn't hint at the failures; it's computed _from_ them and then smooths them out of sight.
2. **recall@100: the median query is perfect, the mean says 0.765.** The typical query recalls _every_ relevant doc (p50 = 1.000), yet a hard tail flooring at p10 = 0.126 drags the average down to 0.765 — a number that describes neither the typical query (1.0) nor the failing tail (0.13).
3. **The mean's error doesn't even keep a stable sign.** On recall@10 it _overstates_ the median (0.563 vs 0.500 — easy, high-recall queries pull it up); on NDCG@10 it _understates_ it (0.526 vs 0.554 — the zero tail pulls it down). It biases in different directions on the same run, so you can't correct for it in your head.

The full percentile spread:

## It hides your improvements, too

The mean doesn't just hide the distribution — it hides your _gains_. When a method genuinely gets better, the mean barely twitches, and the twitch is unintelligible: "+0.019 NDCG" is nothing you can feel or reason about.

Take a real improvement — plain word BM25, then add a Porter stemmer and a stopword list (`word_en`). It moves mean NDCG@10 by **+0.019**, a bump you'd squint at. But look where that improvement actually lands in the distribution:

Same gain, read three ways: the mean smears it across every query and reports **+0.022**; the median reads it where the queries can actually move and reports **+0.100**. The p75 bar is missing because it's pinned at 1.0 — a saturated percentile can't show a gain at all. The same story across all three metrics:

| metric     | mean Δ | where it shows up                                                                                                     |
| ---------- | -----: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| NDCG@10    | +0.019 | p25 / p50 / p75 all move ~2× the mean (+0.037 to +0.045)                                                              |
| recall@10  | +0.022 | the median query's recall@10 jumps **0.50 → 0.60** — a **+0.100 move, 4.6× the mean**                                 |
| recall@100 | +0.028 | median is already 1.0 (stuck), so the gain lands in **p25: 0.50 → 0.63 (+0.126, 4.5×)** — the hard queries got better |

Observations:

1. **The improvement is 2–5× bigger in the percentile where the mass can move.** "Half the queries' recall@10 went from 0.50 to 0.60" is a real, legible win; the mean's +0.022 is that same win smeared into noise. The mean is tiny _because_ it averages the gain across all the saturated queries that can't move — the stuck-at-zero failures and the already-perfect ones.
2. **Which percentile carries the gain shifts by metric.** On recall@100 the median is already perfect, so the improvement lands in the lower quartiles; on recall@10 it lands at the median. Read the percentile where the live mass is, not a fixed one.
3. **The honest catch: a saturated percentile is dead.** p10 for NDCG@10 is pinned at 0.000 (a tenth of queries fail no matter what), p50 for recall@100 pinned at 1.0. There a percentile moves nothing while the mean at least creeps — so percentiles aren't _uniformly_ more sensitive, the one in the live region is, by a lot.

## Why p10, not p90

Latency and quality are the same problem pointed at opposite tails. For latency, higher is worse, so you watch the _high_ end — p90, p99. For quality, higher is better, so the tail that hurts is the _low_ end: **p10, the worst-query floor**. Same instinct, mirrored.

And it _is_ instinct for latency. I timed this BM25 baseline at **3.9ms p50, 8.6ms p90** — nobody would let me publish just the 5ms mean, because the p90 is the request a real user is waiting on. p10 quality is that same user, on the tail that actually hurts. We just forget to report it.

## The ask

Report the median and the p10, not the mean. If you must keep the mean for backward comparison, put it in a fourth column — don't let it be the only one. Better still, show the full per-query distribution: a histogram, or the five-number summary. It costs nothing. You already computed every per-query score in order to average them; you're just throwing the distribution away at the last step.

Concretely, for any retrieval eval:

- **p50** — the typical query. This is your headline, not the mean.
- **p10** — the worst-query floor. This is the number that decides whether users trust the system.
- **mean** — only if a baseline you're comparing against reports it, and even then, next to p50, never instead of it.

One number for search quality is malpractice. We figured this out for latency a decade ago and never looked back. Quality deserves the same.

(This fell out of benchmarking [token search](/blog/token-search) — I was macro-averaging NDCG across datasets and realised the average was quietly deciding my conclusions for me.)

## Key Takeaways

- On 12 NanoBEIR datasets, a plain BM25 baseline scores mean NDCG@10 **0.526** and recall@10 **0.563**, but the **p10 of both is exactly 0.000** — more than one in ten queries retrieves nothing relevant, and the mean hides every one.
- recall@100 makes it sharper: the median query is **perfect (1.000)** while the mean is dragged to **0.765** by a tail flooring at p10 = 0.126. The average describes neither the typical query nor the failing tail — it's a number no real query got.
- The mean hides your _improvements_, not just the distribution: a real gain (adding a stemmer + stopwords) moves mean NDCG@10 by a forgettable **+0.019**, but the median recall@10 jumps **0.50 → 0.60 (+0.100, ~5× the mean's move)** — legible in the percentile where the mass can move, invisible in the average.
- The mean is dominated by easy queries and its error doesn't even keep a stable sign across metrics, so you can't mentally correct for it — the same reason nobody reports average latency.
- For quality, higher is better, so the tail that hurts is the _low_ end: watch **p10**, the worst-query floor, the mirror of p90/p99 latency. We already report the same BM25 run's latency as p50/p90 (3.9ms / 8.6ms) by instinct.
- Report median + p10 (keep the mean only for comparison), or the full distribution. You already computed every per-query score — don't throw the shape away.
