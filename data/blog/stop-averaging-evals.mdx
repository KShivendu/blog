> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

Adding a #[stemmer](it cuts words down to a shared root, so returns becomes return) and a #[stopword list](it drops common words like the, of and in) is standard advice for keyword search. I got a gain of +3.9 in mean #[NDCG@10](a 0 to 100 score for one query, checked against docs that humans labelled relevant. 100 means the most relevant docs sit at the top of the results, 0 means none made the top 10. Scores here are ×100 of the usual 0 to 1 scale, the way IR papers report them) on [DBPedia](https://huggingface.co/datasets/BeIR/dbpedia-entity), which feels like a clear win. But 29% of queries got worse, and `imdb batman returns` started returning _Return of the Joker_ instead of _Batman Returns_.

Any serious team tracks latency as p50, p90, p99. Reporting just "our average latency is 40ms" is naive, because it hides the p99 150ms tail that's hurting real users. Yet we report search relevance as a single average NDCG all the time, and somehow that's normal. Relevance has a tail too, and the average hides it.

I'll use search as the example, but this applies to any eval with graded scores, like LLM-judge ratings.

## Average can't represent the distribution

I ran a plain #[BM25](the standard ranking function for keyword search) baseline (`word`: lowercase and split into words, nothing else) on the full #[DBPedia-entity](a BEIR retrieval benchmark: 4.6M Wikipedia entity pages, title plus abstract, and 400 queries with graded relevance labels) set: 4.6M Wikipedia pages and 400 queries, with about 16 relevant pages per query. Instead of averaging each metric, I calculated percentiles over the per-query scores. The mean NDCG@10 is **27.1**, which reads like every query finds a couple of the right pages. In reality, 25.5% of queries retrieve #[nothing relevant](nothing the dataset's judges marked relevant. On these zero queries, 68% of the top-10 pages were never judged at all, so some zeros are missing labels, not failures. The stemmed run also lands on judged pages more often, 38% unjudged against 43%, which flatters it a little) in the top 10, and the average hides this truth.

Let's look at the distribution.

- A perfect score is rare (1.25% of queries), because the top 10 must hold the highest-graded pages, in grade order.

## The relevance tail

Latency and relevance are the same problem pointed at opposite tails. For latency, higher is worse, so you watch the high end: p90, p99. For relevance, higher is better, so the tail that hurts is the low end. That's the **relevance tail**, the weaker half of your queries. p25 is the score a quarter of your queries get or worse, so it's the number I watch.

Report p25, not p10. p10 pins at 0 once more than 10% of queries fail, and 25.5% do here, so it can't show the tail moving. For plain BM25 even p25 sits at 0 here. It moves once a change pushes the zero-rate (the share of queries with nothing relevant in the top 10) under 25%, and the stemmer below does exactly that.

## Understanding impact of your changes

Take the stemmer from the start: plain word BM25, then apply a Porter stemmer and a stopword list (`word_en`). It moves mean NDCG@10 by **+3.9**, and that gain is real (a paired t-test gives t = 5.4). The mean is right, but it misses that a quarter of the queries don't move and 29% get worse.

(Setup: BM25 with k1=1.5, b=0.75 and Lucene IDF, over each page's title and abstract. `word` lowercases and splits on word characters. `word_en` also drops NLTK's English stopword list and applies NLTK's Porter stemmer.)

### Which queries moved

**p50/p25/mean give you a quick read**. But if you wanna drill down and properly compare two methods, you should look into exact queries that are improved/degraded.

That's why I like to bucket the exact deltas per query for my metrics and use that to find queries to focus on:

Observations:

- 47% of queries got better, 29% got worse and the rest didn't move (**186 wins, 115 losses, 99 ties**). That's why the mean goes up, but only by 3.9.
- Most of the ties score 0 both before and after (59 of the 99).
- The stemmer and stopword list rescued **43 queries from 0**, like `Which airports are located in California, USA?` (0 to 89.1), and sent 17 to 0, like `mason ohio`. That moves the zero-rate from 25.5% to 19.0%.

Some of the queries it broke:

- `imdb batman returns` went from **64.3 to 22.9** on NDCG@10. The stemmer turns `returns` into `return`, so three _Return of the Joker_ pages now take the top 3. _Batman Returns_ falls from rank 1 to 4, and _Batman: The Dark Knight Returns (film)_, the page the judges rated most relevant, falls from rank 2 to 8.
- `mason ohio` went from **38.5 to 0**. The query doesn't change at all, since neither word gets stemmed. The index does: Porter turns "Masonic" into `mason`, so Masonic temples across Ohio crowd the top 10 and push out the town.
- `organic food advantages disadvantages` went from **39.4 to 6.9**. `organic` becomes #[organ](Porter gives organic, organism, organization and organ the same stem), and the top results turn into pages like _Revealed comparative advantage_.

## So what should we actually track?

The goal is to see the distribution. A few numbers give you most of it, and they come from the per-query scores you already have. (No labels yet? Hand-label 50 to 100 real queries, or use an LLM judge.)

- **p50** shows how the typical query does, and it often catches a difference the mean hides. In a recent experiment, two approaches landed within 0.5 on the mean but 3 points apart on the median. That helped me decide which one to go ahead with.
- **p25 and the zero-rate** show how the worst queries do, and that's often where you might wanna spend effort. A query going from 0 to 40 means someone now finds an answer. A query going from 70 to 80 means a good result got a little better.
- **The mean** stays, so you can compare with papers and other runs.

Percentiles need queries that can score many different values. DBPedia has about 16 relevant pages per query, so its scores spread out. On sets where most queries have one relevant doc, like #[NQ](Natural Questions: real Google search questions, each with one Wikipedia answer page), about half the queries score exactly 0. Their median rarely moves, and only in jumps. There, the zero-rate and the better / same / worse split do the job.

This is the table I print after every run while iterating. For this post's change it looks like this:

```md
| analyzer | NDCG@10 p25 | p50   | mean  | zero-rate | better / same / worse |
| -------- | ----------- | ----- | ----- | --------- | --------------------- |
| word     | 0.00        | 21.13 | 27.06 | 25.5%     |                       |
| word_en  | 6.94        | 27.50 | 30.94 | 19.0%     | 46.5% / 24.8% / 28.8% |
```

When something in it moves, I ask an LLM to surface the worst queries and look into them. That's how this run turned up `organic` turning into `organ` and the Joker taking over _Batman Returns_.

> **Beyond search:** any eval with graded per-item scores (an LLM judge's rubric, a similarity score, a per-query metric) hides its tail in the mean the same way.#[\*](Binary pass/fail evals hide less in the mean, but you still want the flip counts: how many items went from pass to fail and from fail to pass.)

Mean alone can't capture a distribution. We realised this for latency decades ago, and search relevance deserves the same. So please look into your distribution and your queries.

## Key Takeaways

- **A good average can hide queries that are hurting real users.** Plain BM25 averages 27.1 NDCG@10 on DBPedia, yet 25.5% of queries find nothing relevant in the top 10.
- **Even standard advice isn't a free lunch.** A stemmer and stopword list move mean NDCG@10 by +3.9, yet 29% of queries get worse. `imdb batman returns` drops from 64.3 to 22.9 because `returns` now matches _Return of the Joker_.
- **Report p50, p25 and the zero-rate next to the mean, then look into the queries that moved.** p25 and the zero-rate show how the worst queries do, which is often where the effort should go.
