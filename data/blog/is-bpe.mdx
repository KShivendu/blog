> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

`the` and `·the` are two different tokens in GPT-2's vocabulary. So are `and` and `·and`, `for` and `·for`, `that` and `·that`. BPE glues the leading space onto the front of a word, so every common word occupies **two** slots, one for when it follows a space and one for when it doesn't. Retrain BPE on 20 MB of Python at 65,536 slots and **45.9% of the vocabulary** goes to pairs like that.

<SpaceSlotHero />

The space is not just the most common character in English text, it's also the cheapest to guess. Under an order-5 character model fitted on one half of C4 and scored on the other, a space costs **1.808 bits** against **2.592** for `e`, the next cheapest character appearing at least a thousand times. On Python it's 1.719 against 3.945. Rank one on frequency and rank one on predictability, in both corpora.

**This is not a GPT-2 quirk.** I checked nine shipped vocabularies across three vendors and both space conventions, and every one holds `the` and `·the` as two separate tokens. Same for `and`, `for`, `that`, `value`, `count` and eight more everyday words I tried.

It is not a rare event either. That 45.9% counts slots. Counting instead how often the duplication is actually hit, by tokenizing 20 MB of C4 and asking what share of the emitted tokens are of a string the vocabulary holds both ways:

```
                                    of emitted tokens, a
                                    string stored twice

r50k          GPT-2, GPT-3                 78.4%
cl100k        GPT-3.5, GPT-4               79.7%
o200k         GPT-4o, o1, GPT-5            80.8%
Qwen3         Qwen                         78.4%
DeepSeek V2   DeepSeek                     81.1%
SmolLM2       HuggingFace                  78.0%
Gemma 2       Google                       87.1%
```

Four out of five, everywhere. Gemma 2 is the worst at **87.1%**, and it uses SentencePiece rather than byte-level BPE, so this outlives the choice of space marker.

So why are we storing it at all, let alone twice?

## The idea: mark the absence, not the presence

A space is the **default** separator between tokens and is never stored. Only its absence is marked, with a `#`. I'll call the scheme ISBPE, for implicit-space BPE.

```
'the cat sat'     ->  the | cat | sat
'hello . world'   ->  hello | . | world
'a, b) c: d'      ->  a | #, | b | #) | c | #: | d
'tokenization'    ->  token | #ization
```

Decoding is one rule: emit a space before each token unless it's the first one, or it carries a `#`, or the previous token was whitespace.

Two properties I kept deliberately, because I tried dropping both and both were bad:

- **Pretokenization stays GPT-2's regex.** Punctuation, digits and letters never mix in one token, and no token spans a word boundary. Removing that barrier is what [SuperBPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13423) does, and it gains 19% on English. I don't want a tokenizer where `of the ` is one symbol, and that gain doesn't transfer across domains anyway.
- **Every byte stays pinned in every flag combination** before training and can never be evicted. That's what makes byte fallback, and therefore losslessness, structural rather than hoped-for. Note that this costs me real budget: ISBPE pins 1,024 byte forms (256 bytes x 4 flag combinations) where BPE pins 256, inside the same 65,536.

All numbers below are against BPE retrained on the same corpus, at the same vocabulary size, with the same GPT-2 pretokenization regex and all 256 byte forms pinned. I verified my regex produces a byte-identical pretoken sequence to the original GPT-2 one over 800 KB of mixed code and prose.

## The failure that pointed at the real fix

Here's where the first version breaks. A word needs **both** forms to be position-independent, and its counts split by context, so a word that mostly appears after a space only ever earns its plain form:

```
'get_attraction'   ->  get | #_ | #att | #raction
```

`attraction` is in the vocabulary. `#attraction` is not, because in prose `attraction` almost always follows a space. So inside `get_attraction` the word shatters.

I tried the obvious fix first, which is to force every string into both forms. That halves the number of distinct strings from 57,966 to 32,768, and it does fix `get_attraction`. It also evicts `reconnaissance`, `Nero`, `Squadron` and `colossal`. Net **-1.9% on C4** and **-4.7% on WikiText**. Wrong trade.

The fix that worked came from asking which side of the boundary should carry the mark.

## Let the left token declare it

Instead of the word carrying `#`, let the thing **before** it say "no space after me". I write that as a trailing `#`:

<FlagSide />

`split` is now the plain token, the same one used in ` split`. Which side carries the flag is decided by what's on the right: **a word on the right means the flag goes left.**

The reason this is exact rather than a heuristic is structural. A word pretoken is ` ?\p{L}++`, which is **maximal**, so the character before a spaceless word can never be a letter. It has to be punctuation, a digit, or one of the seven contraction strings the GPT-2 regex splits off, and all of those sit on the left where they can carry the flag. I measured the leak: **206 boundaries out of 805,820** on code, every one of them a contraction, and [there's a free fix for those](#gpt-2s-regex-eats-the-first-letter-of-a-quoted-string) further down.

So **a word never needs a `#` form at all.** On code that makes 12,439 word slots reclaimable and pools **780,827 token occurrences** onto single ids. `format` stops being 1,433 uses in one slot plus 5,740 in another and becomes 7,173 in one.

Note that the trained vocabulary banks about two thirds of that. Measured after training, strings held in two forms fall from 15,046 to **6,214**, and distinct strings held rise from 50,490 to **58,517**, a net gain of roughly 8,000 slots rather than the full 12,439. The merge process spends the rest elsewhere.

**Which side you pick is not a question I invented.** [Jacobs and Pinter (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01561) asked exactly this for SentencePiece, word-initial mark against word-final, and settled it with a sweep. What's different here is that I'm marking absence rather than presence, so the answer is forced by the pretokenizer wherever a word follows, and the residual is a count I can state rather than a preference.

## GPT-4's regex deletes the same token and pays for it in slots

Everything above compares against BPE with GPT-2's 2019 regex. GPT-4 and everything after it use a different one, and it goes after the same waste from the other side. So the fair question is what marking the absence buys once the baseline is current.

GPT-2 lets only a space begin a word. cl100k lets any character that is not a letter, a digit or a newline begin one:

```
                  GPT-2's regex          cl100k's regex

'lang.split(x)'   lang | . | split       lang | .split
                  | ( | x | )            | (x | )
```

Each glued character saves a token. In that one line it saves two, the dot and the open paren. The cost is that a word now needs its own slot for every character that can precede it. cl100k holds **sixteen** forms of `value`:

```
value    ·value   .value   _value   (value   )value
,value   -value   /value   :value   "value   'value
=value   [value   $value   \tvalue
```

GPT-2 holds two. Retrain both at the same 65,536 slots on the same 20 MB of Python and the glue takes multi-form slots from **44.2%** of the vocabulary to **54.1%**.

In the shipped vocabularies the same shows up as 47.8% for cl100k against 30.6% for GPT-2. o200k reads 27.8%, which is lower than GPT-2's, because its 200,000 slots carry a long dead tail that dilutes the denominator. The per-word count is the cleaner read, and o200k holds the same sixteen forms of `value` that cl100k does.

So both designs delete a token and both pay for it in slots. Trained on the same 20 MB of Python at the same 65,536 vocabulary, they turn out to be worth almost the same amount. Changing only GPT-2's word clause to allow the glue takes 336,736 tokens down to **303,927**, a 9.74% cut. The dual flag takes it to **305,810**, a 9.18% cut. Two different mechanisms, 0.6 points apart.

**On sequence length the modern regex wins and my scheme does not.** 283,269 tokens and 2.711x compression against ISBPE's 305,810 and 2.655x. The 9.4% I quoted earlier is against a pretokenizer from 2019.

It splits the other way on the two numbers that are not sequence length. The first is bits per byte, the held-out cross-entropy divided by the byte count of the text rather than by the token count, which is the only way to compare two tokenizers that chop text at different rates. ISBPE reaches **1.3205 against cl100k's 1.3937**, which is 5.25% better, and [later on](#does-any-of-this-help-an-llm) it is the number I argue is the one to watch.

The other is word integrity. Take the 3,000 most frequent camelCase identifiers in the held-out Python and encode each one on its own. cl100k returns **2.93%** of them as a single token and ISBPE returns **10.28%**. That is the friendliest possible test for a scheme whose whole point is one canonical form, and it is a different measurement from the in-context Java count in [what it costs](#what-it-costs), where BPE is 1.01 points ahead. The glue buys its tokens by spending slots on `(self`, `_name` and `.get`, and those are slots a long identifier never gets.

### Take both

The obvious move is to put the flag on top of cl100k's regex, and it does not work. The dual-flag mark needs the punctuation to be its own pretoken so it has somewhere to sit. Under cl100k, `.split` is one pretoken, so there is no left token to mark.

I tried taking one of cl100k's other clauses for free instead. It also absorbs the line ending into the punctuation run before it, which is worth 6.1 points to plain BPE and never touches the word clause. On ISBPE it emits **312,522** tokens against 305,810, which is 2.2% worse. It bought 2,237 slots and only **532** of them ever fire on held-out text, and strings held both bare and newline-suffixed went from 4 to **916**. The clause recreated the duplication the flag had just removed.

So a merge has to be earned. The two fixes are not alternatives: the flag removes the second form of every word, the glue removes the separate punctuation token, and nothing stops doing both.

Keep GPT-2's word clause and the dual flag exactly as they are. Train at 65,536 minus K. Then spend those K slots on the most frequent punctuation-word pairs measured on the training corpus.

The word keeps one canonical form. `split` is still a single slot, and `.split` is an extra token that had to fire often enough to earn one. That is the whole difference from cl100k, where the glue sits in the regex so `value` never exists on its own.

| arm, 20 MB Python, V=65,536 | tokens      | compression | bits/byte  | whole alone |
| --------------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| BPE + GPT-2 regex           | 336,736     | 2.428x      | 1.3864     | 7.00%       |
| BPE + cl100k regex          | 283,269     | 2.711x      | 1.3937     | 2.93%       |
| ISBPE dual-flag             | 305,810     | 2.655x      | **1.3205** | **10.28%**  |
| ISBPE + 500 earned glue     | 283,386     | 2.751x      | 1.3255     | 10.02%      |
| ISBPE + 4,000 earned glue   | **271,120** | **2.827x**  | 1.3352     | 10.02%      |

Two notes on that table. Bits per byte here is measured on the frequency-remapped id stream, so the absolute numbers sit a little below the [vocabulary sweep below](#the-same-win-read-as-vocabulary-size), 1.3864 against 1.3977 for BPE. The gap between arms is the same either way, 4.8% against 4.75%. And "whole alone" is the standalone encoding described above, not the in-context Java count.

Observations:

- **500 slots is 0.76% of the vocabulary.** It lands 117 tokens behind cl100k, 283,386 against 283,269, with better compression (2.751x against 2.711x) and 3.4x as many identifiers kept whole.
- **4,000 slots beats cl100k by 4.3% on tokens and 4.3% on compression.** The gain is broad: per 4 KB chunk the median goes from cl100k's 2.755x to 2.888x, and the hard tenth from 2.410x to 2.528x.
- **Bits per byte is the number that moves the wrong way**, 1.3205 to 1.3352, and identifiers whole slips a little too, 10.28% to 10.02%. Both are still far ahead of cl100k's 1.3937 and 2.93%. On English the same 4,000 slots move bits per byte the other way, 2.1090 to 2.1070, so I would not call it a rule.
- **English saturates almost at once.** 250 slots gets 275,113 tokens and 4,000 gets 274,414, which is 699 tokens for sixteen times the budget. The glue fires on **2.4%** of English word pretokens against **26.3%** on code, and the leading characters there are apostrophes and hyphens rather than `_` and `(`.

Two limits worth naming. The merges are single level, so `.` and `get` become `.get` but `.get` cannot merge again, which understates the result. And the earned glue is measured but not shipped. It patches the vocabulary after training rather than changing the trainer, so the library below does not do it yet.

## Where the win actually comes from

The driver is compound identifiers. Line starts are the obvious guess and they are only 1,495 of the 30,926 tokens saved, under 5%.

```
'self.accountemail:'
  ISBPE  #account 13.1 bits   #email 16.5          2 tokens, 29.6 bits
  BPE    ac 16.5  coun 24.9  te 15.6  mail 18.8    4 tokens, 75.8 bits
```

After `self.` there is no space, so BPE needs the bare `account`, which lost its slot to the space-prefixed form, and it shatters. ISBPE's `#account` exists because one marked form pools every spaceless context.

It cuts the other way where the glued piece is code-specific rather than an English word. `Segments` and `Contour` appear almost only glued, so BPE holds each whole while ISBPE divides its budget into `S | #eg | #ments` and `Cont | #our`. Per line on held-out Python, ISBPE is cheaper on 59% of lines and dearer on 26%.

## It transfers to text it never saw

Corpus-fitted tokenizers usually give their gains back the moment you point them at different text. This one keeps them, and for the same reason it wins in domain.

Train the vocabulary on 20 MB of Python, then run it over English prose it has never seen. One real sentence out of the held-out C4 file:

```
'This is a lot of what I do with the college students I see.'

  BPE          19 tokens
  single-flag  18
  dual-flag    16
```

The word `students` carries most of that gap. A code corpus does contain the word, in identifiers, comments and docstrings, but almost always glued to something rather than sitting after a space. BPE therefore learns the glued form and never earns a space-prefixed one, so in prose the word shatters:

```
  BPE          st | udent | s      3 pieces
  single-flag  stud | #ents        2
  dual-flag    students            1, the word whole
```

Across the whole held-out file, every arm trained on the same 20 MB:

| vocabulary trained on | tested on         | dual       | single | BPE        | dual vs BPE |
| --------------------- | ----------------- | ---------- | ------ | ---------- | ----------- |
| code                  | Python, in domain | **2.655x** | 2.609x | 2.428x     | **+9.37%**  |
| code                  | C4, unseen        | **2.035x** | 1.992x | 1.995x     | **+1.97%**  |
| English               | C4, in domain     | **2.700x** | 2.685x | 2.677x     | **+0.86%**  |
| English               | Python, unseen    | **1.695x** | 1.653x | 1.672x     | **+1.36%**  |
| English               | WikiText, unseen  | 2.387x     | 2.438x | **2.444x** | **-2.35%**  |

Observations:

- **Dual-flag wins both of the unseen rows that are real text.** A code vocabulary reads English 1.97% better than BPE does, and an English vocabulary reads Python 1.36% better.
- **Single-flag loses both**, 1.992x against BPE's 1.995x on C4 and 1.653x against 1.672x on Python. In domain the two schemes sit within 2% of each other. Out of domain only the two-sided mark survives, which is the clearest argument for putting the mark on the left.
- **WikiText is the exception and it costs 2.35%.** Its detokenized punctuation is spaced, which is a corpus artifact rather than a property of prose. More on that in [what it costs](#what-it-costs).

## What it costs

A post called "Improving BPE" that lists no losses is an advertisement. Three, all measured:

- **WikiText loses 2.35%.** Its detokenized punctuation is spaced, so `,` and `.` appear 23,262 times against C4's 136. The "no space after" forms almost never fire and the slots spent on them are dead. That's a corpus artifact rather than a property of prose, but I have no second clean English holdout to prove it.
- **Word boundaries get slightly worse.** On 5 MB of held-out Java, scoring every boundary a programmer marked with a capital, plain BPE keeps 73.11% of those words whole against ISBPE's 72.10%. It goes both ways. ISBPE splits `tagName` into `tag | #Name` and `CodeType` into `Code | #Type`, where BPE holds each whole. BPE splits `hasProperty` into `has | Property` and `FedoraResource` into `Fed | or | aResource`, where ISBPE holds each whole. The net is 1.01 points against ISBPE. If your downstream task wants morpheme-aligned pieces, measure this before adopting the scheme.
- **Digits are marginally worse.** 83,003 digit tokens against BPE's 80,432 on English, once counted fairly.

On code the sign never flips, since 45.9% of that vocabulary was duplicate pairs to begin with. This is a scheme for vocabularies large enough to have been wasting slots in the first place.

## Does any of this help an LLM?

Compression ratio is the wrong metric for that question, so I measured **bits per byte**, defined above. That normalisation is the whole game. A word-level tokenizer has the lowest bits per **token** of anything I measured, and the highest bits per **byte**. It chops text finer, so each guess is easier and there are more of them.

I expected a token-count win to show up proportionally in bits per byte. It does not, and there is no fixed conversion either. Across variant, corpus and instrument the surviving fraction runs from **7% to over 100%**, so there is no factor that turns one into the other.

<details>
<summary>The full survival table, and why no single number comes out of it</summary>

| variant, corpus    | token win | survives, order-3 n-gram | survives, xz |
| ------------------ | --------- | ------------------------ | ------------ |
| single-flag, code  | 8.96%     | 15%                      | 15%          |
| dual-flag, code    | 9.18%     | **7%**                   | **53%**      |
| dual-flag, English | 0.40%     | 165%                     | 308%         |

Observations:

- **Any single number you pull out of this table is one cell**, and quoting it as a rule is how you end up telling people to divide token-count claims by seven.
- **For single-flag on code the instrument makes no difference**, 15% either way, despite one model seeing two tokens of context and the other seeing the whole file.
- **One cell moves, and it's the interesting one.** Dual-flag on code goes 7% to 53%. It buys longer literal repeats in the id stream, which a long-range coder feeds on. The cost is splitting `.` and `.#` into separate conditioning contexts, and those contexts are all a two-token model can see.
- **The English row has a tiny denominator.** A 0.40% token win is about 1,100 tokens, so the size of that ratio means little. Both instruments agree on its sign.

</details>

A short-context proxy also can't rank the two variants. Measured with an order-3 n-gram, single-flag beats dual-flag on bits per byte by **0.75%**, which reads like a clean verdict. Three long-range coders reverse it, all in dual-flag's favour. xz says **-3.51%**, bz2 **-2.12%** and zlib **-2.36%**. So the ranking is not stable under the choice of instrument, which is a different claim from saying the n-gram is wrong. Settling it needs a training run I haven't done.

Note the obvious counter. [Goldman et al. (2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.134/) pretrained models and found compression does correlate with downstream performance. Where we appear to disagree, theirs is the stronger evidence, because they trained models and I ran n-grams and compressors.

## GPT-2's regex eats the first letter of a quoted string

Those 206 leaking boundaries are all the same bug, and fixing it moves compression by under 0.01% either way.

```
pretokens of  b'test'   ->   [b'b', b"'t", b'est', b"'"]
```

GPT-2's regex offers the contraction `'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)` as a **standalone** alternative, so it fires inside quoted string literals and eats the first letter of the word. Every one of the 206 leaks is this:

```
count   left   word           the literal it came from
   32   't     h              a 'h...' string
    5   're    sult           'result'
    5   's     ecurityOrig    'securityOrigin'
    5   't     ype            'type'
    3   'd     ata            'data'
```

A lookbehind doesn't help, since the character before the quote (`b`) is a letter. [o200k](https://github.com/openai/tiktoken) doesn't fix it either, it attaches the contraction as a word suffix and gives `b't | est | '`. What works is requiring the contraction not to be followed by another letter:

```
'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)   ->   '(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)(?!\p{L})
```

`don't` still matches. `b'test'` falls through to punctuation and `test` comes out whole. Pretoken **counts are identical** on 20 MB of code and of C4, only 206 and 34 boundaries move, and compression changes by +0.004% and -0.007%. With the fix the leak count on code is **0**, so the structural argument above has no exceptions left.

## What I'd actually use

I packaged the implementation as a small library with both modes:

```python
from isbpe import ISBPE

tok = ISBPE(mode="dual").train(corpus_bytes, vocab_size=65536)
ids = tok.encode("self.fireEvent")
tok.decode(ids)                      # exact bytes back, always

tok.explain("self.fireEvent")
# self.fireEvent -> self | #.# | fire | #Event
```

`mode="dual"` is the one to use, and the contraction fix is the default pattern. Losslessness is verified on three corpora plus 2,000 random byte strings per mode, and the packaged library reproduces every number in this post to the token except the earned-glue rows, which patch the vocabulary after training and are not in the library.

The honest limitations. A custom tokenizer produces ids no existing model can read, so if your reader is an LLM you need the lab to ship the vocabulary. Everything here is one 20 MB corpus per domain, one held-out file, one deterministic build, with no variance reported. And the headline comparison is against GPT-2's 2019 pretokenizer, which [a modern one beats on its own](#gpt-4s-regex-deletes-the-same-token-and-pays-for-it-in-slots). For a storage layer where you control both ends, none of that applies and you can use it today.

## Key Takeaways

- **Every tokenizer stores the same word twice**, and it is most of what they emit. Nine shipped vocabularies across three vendors all hold `the` and `·the` as separate tokens, and four out of five tokens they emit on C4 are of a string stored twice like that.
- **Marking the absence removes that second form. A modern pretokenizer removes a different token instead.** Gluing punctuation onto the word is worth 9.74% on held-out Python, and the dual flag is worth 9.18%. Two mechanisms, 0.6 points apart, aimed at the same waste.
- **Doing both beats doing either.** ISBPE plus 4,000 slots of earned glue reaches **271,120 tokens** and **2.827x**, against the modern baseline's 283,269 and 2.711x, while keeping 3.4x as many identifiers whole. Measured by patching the vocabulary after training rather than in the shipped library.
- **The same win, spent differently, is an 8x smaller vocabulary.** ISBPE needs **7,658 slots** to emit as few tokens on held-out Python as BPE with GPT-2's regex needs 65,536 for. Measured in bits per byte it needs **5,149**.
- **A flag pays only when it removes a form from the vocabulary.** The space flag deletes a form per word and wins. Absorbing the line ending into the punctuation before it adds a form instead, and that cost 2.2%.
