> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

`the` and `·the` are two different tokens in GPT-2's vocabulary. So are `and` and `·and`, `for` and `·for`, `that` and `·that`. BPE glues the leading space onto the front of a word, so every common word occupies **two** slots, one for when it follows a space and one for when it doesn't. Retrain BPE on 20 MB of Python at 65,536 slots and **45.9% of the vocabulary** goes to pairs like that.

<SpaceSlotHero />

The space is not just the most common character in English text, it's also the cheapest to guess. Under an order-5 character model fitted on one half of C4 and scored on the other, a space costs **1.808 bits** against **2.592** for `e`, the next cheapest character appearing at least a thousand times. On Python it's 1.719 against 3.945. Rank one on frequency and rank one on predictability, in both corpora.

So why are we storing it at all, let alone twice?

## The idea: mark the absence, not the presence

A space is the **default** separator between tokens and is never stored. Only its absence is marked, with a `#`.

```
'the cat sat'     ->  the | cat | sat
'hello . world'   ->  hello | . | world
'a, b) c: d'      ->  a | #, | b | #) | c | #: | d
'tokenization'    ->  token | #ization
```

Decoding is one rule: emit a space before each token unless it's the first one, or it carries a `#`, or the previous token was whitespace.

Two properties I kept deliberately, because I tried dropping both and both were bad:

- **Pretokenization stays GPT-2's regex.** Punctuation, digits and letters never mix in one token, and no token spans a word boundary. Removing that barrier is what [SuperBPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13423) does, and it gains 19% on English. I don't want a tokenizer where `of the ` is one symbol, and that gain doesn't transfer across domains anyway.
- **Every byte stays pinned in every flag combination** before training and can never be evicted. That's what makes byte fallback, and therefore losslessness, structural rather than hoped-for. Note that this costs me real budget: ISBPE pins 1,024 byte forms (256 bytes x 4 flag combinations) where BPE pins 256, inside the same 65,536.

All numbers below are against BPE retrained on the same corpus, at the same vocabulary size, with the same GPT-2 pretokenization regex and all 256 byte forms pinned. I verified my regex produces a byte-identical pretoken sequence to the original GPT-2 one over 800 KB of mixed code and prose.

## The failure that pointed at the real fix

Here's where the first version breaks. A word needs **both** forms to be position-independent, and its counts split by context, so a word that mostly appears after a space only ever earns its plain form:

```
'get_attraction'   ->  get | #_ | #att | #raction
```

`attraction` is in the vocabulary. `#attraction` is not, because in prose `attraction` almost always follows a space. So inside `get_attraction` the word shatters.

I tried the obvious fix first, which is to force every string into both forms. That halves the number of distinct strings from 57,966 to 32,768, and it does fix `get_attraction`. It also evicts `reconnaissance`, `Nero`, `Squadron` and `colossal`. Net **-1.9% on C4** and **-4.7% on WikiText**. Wrong trade.

The fix that worked came from asking which side of the boundary should carry the mark.

## Let the left token declare it

Instead of the word carrying `#`, let the thing **before** it say "no space after me". I write that as a trailing `#`:

<FlagSide />

`split` is now the plain token, the same one used in ` split`. Which side carries the flag is decided by what's on the right: **a word on the right means the flag goes left.**

The reason this is exact rather than a heuristic is structural. A word pretoken is ` ?\p{L}++`, which is **maximal**, so the character before a spaceless word can never be a letter. It has to be punctuation, a digit, or one of the seven contraction strings the GPT-2 regex splits off, and all of those sit on the left where they can carry the flag. I measured the leak: **206 boundaries out of 805,820** on code, every one of them a contraction, and [there's a free fix for those](#gpt-2s-regex-eats-the-first-letter-of-a-quoted-string) further down.

So **a word never needs a `#` form at all.** On code that makes 12,439 word slots reclaimable and pools **780,827 token occurrences** onto single ids. `format` stops being 1,433 uses in one slot plus 5,740 in another and becomes 7,173 in one.

Note that the trained vocabulary banks about two thirds of that. Measured after training, strings held in two forms fall from 15,046 to **6,214**, and distinct strings held rise from 50,490 to **58,517**, a net gain of roughly 8,000 slots rather than the full 12,439. The merge process spends the rest elsewhere.

**Which side you pick is not a question I invented.** [Jacobs and Pinter (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01561) asked exactly this for SentencePiece, word-initial mark against word-final, and settled it with a sweep. What's different here is that I'm marking absence rather than presence, so the answer is forced by the pretokenizer wherever a word follows, and the residual is a count I can state rather than a preference.

Observations:

1. **Dual-flag wins on both corpora, and it wins on every statistic.** Toggle the chart: mean, median, the hard chunks and the easy ones all point the same way. Note that these are per-chunk numbers, so they differ slightly from the whole-file ratios (2.655x on code, 2.700x on English) that the rest of the post quotes, since a whole-file ratio is byte-weighted and this is not.
2. **The gain is broad, not carried by a few chunks.** On code the hard tenth of chunks goes 2.183x to 2.361x and the easy tenth 2.691x to 2.989x, so the whole distribution shifts. On English it's +1.2% at p10 and +0.8% at p90, small everywhere rather than large somewhere.
3. **The English win is half id coding.** `+freq` means ids re-ranked by training frequency and packed with streamvbyte, so small ids cost fewer bytes. In raw token counts the two arms are 9.18% and 0.40% apart, against 9.37% and 0.86% here. It's still the first time anything I built beat a properly-trained BPE on English, and it's one run with no confidence interval, so read it as directional.

5. **Code gains 10x more than English**, and the reason is countable: 45.9% of the code vocabulary sits in duplicate pairs against **23.1%** of the English one. English words nearly all carry a space already, so there's less for the left-flag to pool.
6. **It also wins out of domain**, which is where corpus-fitted tokenizers usually give everything back. Dual-flag beats BPE on a code vocabulary tested on C4 (+1.97%) and an English vocabulary tested on Python (+1.36%). Single-flag loses both. In domain the two schemes differ by under 2%, out of domain only the two-sided one transfers.

Compression ratio is one way to spend the freed slots. The other is to not spend them.

## Where the win actually comes from

The obvious explanation is line starts. BPE shatters `else` into `el|se` at the start of a line, because its counts split between `·else` and bare `else`, and code is full of line starts. That effect is real and it's small: attributing the win line by line, those cases come to 1,495 of the 30,926 tokens saved, under 5%.

The driver is compound identifiers:

```
'self.accountemail:'
  ISBPE  #account 13.1 bits   #email 16.5          2 tokens, 29.6 bits
  BPE    ac 16.5  coun 24.9  te 15.6  mail 18.8    4 tokens, 75.8 bits
```

After `self.` there is no space, so BPE needs the bare `account`, which lost its slot to the space-prefixed form, and it shatters. ISBPE's `#account` exists because one marked form pools every spaceless context.

It cuts the other way where the glued piece is code-specific rather than an English word. `Segments`, `Contour` and `Timestamp` appear almost only glued, so BPE holds each as one long bare token while ISBPE divides its budget. Per line on held-out Python, ISBPE is cheaper on 59% of lines and dearer on 26%, with gross flows running 2.2:1 in its favour.

## What it costs

A post called "Improving BPE" that lists no losses is an advertisement. Three, all measured:

- **WikiText loses 2.35%.** Its detokenized punctuation is spaced, so `,` and `.` appear 23,262 times against C4's 136. The "no space after" forms almost never fire and the slots spent on them are dead. That's a corpus artifact rather than a property of prose, but I have no second clean English holdout to prove it.
- **Word boundaries get slightly worse.** On 5 MB of held-out Java, scoring every boundary a programmer marked with a capital, plain BPE keeps 73.11% of those words whole against ISBPE's 72.10%. A word like `Scheduler` earns a slot only in its after-a-space form, so mid-identifier the tokenizer is forced into `S|cheduler`. If your downstream task wants morpheme-aligned pieces, measure this before adopting the scheme.
- **Digits are marginally worse.** 83,003 digit tokens against BPE's 80,432 on English, once counted fairly.

## Does any of this help an LLM?

Compression ratio is the wrong metric for that question, so I measured **bits per byte**: held-out cross-entropy under n-gram models, normalised by the byte count of the same text rather than the token count. That normalisation is the whole game. A word-level tokenizer has the lowest bits per **token** of anything I measured and the highest bits per **byte**, because it chops text finer, so each guess is easier and there are more of them.

I expected a token-count win to show up proportionally in bits per byte. It does not, and there is no fixed conversion either.

| variant, corpus    | token win | survives, order-3 n-gram | survives, xz |
| ------------------ | --------- | ------------------------ | ------------ |
| single-flag, code  | 8.96%     | 15%                      | 15%          |
| dual-flag, code    | 9.18%     | **7%**                   | **53%**      |
| dual-flag, English | 0.40%     | 165%                     | 308%         |

Observations:

- **There is no conversion factor.** The surviving fraction runs from 7% to over 100% depending on the variant, the corpus and the instrument. Any single number you pull out of this table is one cell, and quoting it as a rule is how you end up telling people to divide token-count claims by seven.
- **For single-flag on code the instrument makes no difference**, 15% either way, despite one model seeing two tokens of context and the other seeing the whole file.
- **One cell moves, and it's the interesting one.** Dual-flag on code goes 7% to 53%. Dual-flag buys longer literal repeats in the id stream, which a long-range coder feeds on, at the cost of splitting `.` and `.#` into separate conditioning contexts, which is all a two-token model can see.
- **The English row has a tiny denominator.** A 0.40% token win is about 1,100 tokens, so the size of that ratio means little. Both instruments agree on its sign.

That third point is why a short-context proxy can't rank these two variants. Measured with an order-3 n-gram, single-flag beats dual-flag on bits per byte by **0.75%**, which reads like a clean verdict. Three long-range coders reverse it: xz **-3.51%**, bz2 **-2.12%**, zlib **-2.36%**, all in dual-flag's favour. The ranking is not stable under the choice of instrument, which is different from saying the n-gram is wrong, and settling it needs a training run I haven't done.

Note the obvious counter. [Goldman et al. (2024)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.134/) pretrained models and found compression does correlate with downstream performance. Where we appear to disagree, theirs is the stronger evidence, because they trained models and I ran n-grams and compressors.

<details>
<summary>Capitalisation, priced one way and built three, all lost</summary>

`Attraction` and `attraction` are two slots, and capital-initial entries are **35.5% of the English vocabulary**. So: mark the capital and share the lowercase form.

**A marker token**, `<CAP> attraction`, frees 23,253 slots on English but grows the stream **13.04%**, since English capitalises 13% of its tokens and each one costs a whole extra token. Rejected on arithmetic, never built.

**A case flag inside the token id** costs zero extra tokens and still lost 0.24% on code. It eliminates nothing: `the` and `The` still need two ids, because an id has to determine its output bytes. It only adds a dimension, taking flag combinations per string from 4 to 12, and distinct strings held on code fell from 58,517 to 49,456.

**A mergeable marker token**, so `<CAP>+the` can merge into a single `The` slot built on the existing `the`, is the best of the designs and it's adaptive, since frequent capitals earn a merged slot and rare ones consume no vocabulary. It still lost 6.30%, because `<CAP>` was emitted unmerged **401,589 times, 8.79% of the stream**. English capitalisation is a fat tail of proper nouns, and a marker only pays for words frequent enough to earn a slot, which are exactly the words that were already cheap to store.

The rule that came out of it: **a flag pays when it removes a form from the vocabulary, and costs when it only adds a dimension to the id.** The dual-flag `#` removed a form per word. Case flags remove nothing.

</details>

## GPT-2's regex eats the first letter of a quoted string

Those 206 leaking boundaries are all the same bug, and it's worth fixing on its own terms.

```
pretokens of  b'test'   ->   [b'b', b"'t", b'est', b"'"]
```

GPT-2's regex offers the contraction `'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)` as a **standalone** alternative, so it fires inside quoted string literals and eats the first letter of the word. Every one of the 206 leaks is this:

```
count   left   word           the literal it came from
   32   't     h              a 'h...' string
    5   're    sult           'result'
    5   's     ecurityOrig    'securityOrigin'
    5   't     ype            'type'
    3   'd     ata            'data'
```

A lookbehind doesn't help, since the character before the quote (`b`) is a letter. [o200k](https://github.com/openai/tiktoken) doesn't fix it either, it attaches the contraction as a word suffix and gives `b't | est | '`. What works is requiring the contraction not to be followed by another letter:

```
'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)   ->   '(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)(?!\p{L})
```

`don't` still matches. `b'test'` falls through to punctuation and `test` comes out whole. Pretoken **counts are identical** on 20 MB of code and of C4, only 206 and 34 boundaries move, and compression changes by +0.004% and -0.007%. With the fix the leak count on code is **0**, so the structural argument above has no exceptions left.

## What I'd actually use

I packaged the implementation as a small library with both modes:

```python
from isbpe import ISBPE

tok = ISBPE(mode="dual").train(corpus_bytes, vocab_size=65536)
ids = tok.encode("self.fireEvent")
tok.decode(ids)                      # exact bytes back, always

tok.explain("self.fireEvent")
# self.fireEvent -> self | #.# | fire | #Event
```

`mode="dual"` is the one to use, and the contraction fix is the default pattern. Losslessness is verified on three corpora plus 2,000 random byte strings per mode, and the packaged library reproduces the numbers in this post to the token.

The honest limitations. A custom tokenizer produces ids no existing model can read, so if your reader is an LLM you need the lab to ship the vocabulary. Everything here is one 20 MB corpus per domain, one held-out file, one deterministic build, with no variance reported. And the comparison is against GPT-2's 2019 pretokenizer, not a modern one that caps digit runs. For a storage layer where you control both ends, none of that applies and you can use it today.

## Key Takeaways

- **Marking the absence of a space beats storing it.** ISBPE dual-flag reaches **2.655x on code** and **2.700x on English** against a retrained BPE baseline's 2.428x and 2.677x, and it's the only variant that also transfers out of domain.
- **The same win, spent differently, is an 8x smaller vocabulary.** ISBPE needs **7,658 slots** to emit as few tokens on held-out Python as BPE needs 65,536 for.
- **Which side carries the mark matters more than the inversion.** Putting it on the left token makes "a word never needs a marked form" exact rather than statistical, with a residual of 206 boundaries in 805,820, all contractions and all fixable.
- **A token-count win carries no fixed information about bits per byte.** Across variant, corpus and instrument the surviving fraction runs 7% to over 100%, so there is no factor to convert one into the other.
- **A flag pays only when it removes a form from the vocabulary.** The space flag deleted a form per word and won. Capitalisation flags only add a dimension to the id, and every version I tried lost.
