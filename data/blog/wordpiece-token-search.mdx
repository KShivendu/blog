> Looking for TL;DR? Check [key takeaways](#key-takeaways)

A search index starts with two decisions you make before you have seen a single query: which tokenizer splits the text, and which analyzer normalises it. Lowercase, a stopword list, Porter for English, a segmenter for Chinese. Both decisions get baked into every posting list you write, so changing either one later means reading every document again. Then a neural sparse model arrives, [SPLADE](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3) or an OpenSearch neural sparse encoder, and it emits weights over `bert-base-uncased` wordpieces while your index is keyed by Porter stems. The model cannot score a term it cannot name, so you rebuild.

There is a starting point that survives all of it: index only the wordpiece tokens each document actually contains. BM25 runs on those postings on day one, with no stemmer, no stopword list and no per-language analyzer. The surface word and its stem family are arithmetic on the same postings, so those two extra channels cost no extra index. And when you are ready for the model, it speaks the same vocabulary, so it updates the weights on terms you already have and adds expansion terms into posting lists that already exist. Nothing is rebuilt.

All numbers below are over 13 #[NanoBEIR](small subsampled stand-ins for the BEIR retrieval benchmarks) [collections](https://huggingface.co/collections/zeta-alpha-ai/nanobeir-66e1a0af21dfd93e620cd9f6), 56,723 documents and 649 queries, on `bert-base-uncased` WordPiece. Quality is NDCG@10, which rates the top ten results from 0 to 1 by how close they sit to the ideal ranking, and I report it multiplied by 100 throughout, so **54.12 means an NDCG@10 of 0.5412**. On this benchmark a couple of points is a solid win and a few tenths of a point is near the noise floor. Every fitted weight is held out leave-one-collection-out, so no arm sees its own test collection, and I report the median and the weak-query quartile next to the mean because [the mean hides where a change helps](/blog/relevance-tail).

## Three stages, one set of postings

```
  what you store, once:

    piece       documents that contain it
    token       d7:3   d19:1   d88:1   d204:3
    ##ization   d7:2   d19:1           d204:3
    ...

  stage 1   a BM25 weight per piece                          a formula
  stage 2   + the surface word and its stem family,
            read back off those same lists                   arithmetic
  stage 3   + a model's weight per piece, and its
            expansion terms into lists already open          a model
```

Each stage keeps the vocabulary and the posting lists of the one before it. What changes is the weight attached to a posting, and at the last stage, which documents appear in a list.

## Stage 1: BM25 over the pieces the document has

WordPiece is a fixed function from a string to a list of piece ids. It reads no documents and computes no statistics, so it is the same function on the day you index and two years later:

```
tokenization  ->  token  ##ization
tokens        ->  token  ##s
tokenizer     ->  token  ##izer
```

Every document becomes the multiset of pieces it contains, and a posting exists only where the count is nonzero. That is the whole index. BM25 scores it the usual way, an IDF per piece, term-frequency saturation and length normalisation, and it never learns that `token` and `##ization` came from one word.

In [Token Search](/blog/token-search) I ran BM25 over tiktoken's BPE token IDs, where it matched a plain word analyzer and reached a Porter-stemmed one with two token-native fixes. The tokenizer here is `bert-base-uncased` WordPiece instead, because that is the vocabulary the neural sparse models emit. Plain wordpiece BM25 scores **54.12** over the 649 queries.

The index it builds is the larger of the two you could have chosen:

| structure       | (doc, term) postings | vs word index |
| --------------- | -------------------- | ------------- |
| word index      | 5,069,947            | 1.00x         |
| wordpiece index | 5,593,439            | 1.10x         |
| stem index      | 4,847,061            | 0.96x         |

Observations:

- **Pieces cost 10% more postings than words.** 5,593,439 against 5,069,947, because splitting a word spreads one document's occurrences across more posting lists.
- **The vocabulary moves the other way.** 217,815 piece types against 355,067 word types across the 13 collections. Fewer, longer posting lists is the shape that makes them expensive to store and cheap to derive.
- **Storing two of them roughly doubles the index.** Word plus piece together is 2.10x the word index and word plus stem 1.96x, which is the cost the next section removes.

## Stage 2: the word and its family are arithmetic on those postings

A word posting and a piece posting hold the same information counted two ways, and the tokenizer is the conversion table. Going from words to pieces is a sum:

```
tf_piece(p, d)  =  sum over words v of  count_p(v) * tf_v(d)
```

If a word posting says doc 7 contains `tokenization` three times, then doc 7 contains `token` three times and `##ization` three times from that word alone. `count_p(v)` is how many times piece `p` occurs inside word `v`, so a word like `bookkeeper` splitting into `book ##ke ##eper` is handled, and so is a word whose pieces repeat. I built the piece index directly, built it again by derivation, and compared list against list: **all 217,815 piece posting lists match exactly**, on every collection.

Going the other way, pieces back to words, is the direction this design needs, and it has a trap in it. A short word's piece sequence can be a prefix of a longer word's:

```
token         ->  [token]
tokenization  ->  [token, ##ization]
```

Naive adjacency says the word `token` occurs in every document containing `tokenization`, which is false. The fix is one extra condition: the word `w` with `k` pieces occurs at position `p` only when the pieces match at `p` through `p+k-1` and the token at `p+k` carries no `##` continuation marker. With that check, **all 52,000 word types reconstruct identically** from the positional piece postings. Without it the derivation picks up **83,735** false postings, and the worst single case is the word `un` on Touche2020, which gains **1,565 documents it never appears in**.

Observations:

- **The exact word is worth +1.29.** 54.12 to 55.41, at 134 wins to 76 losses, p=0.0001. Scoring the whole word once, on top of its pieces, restores what the split threw away: that `token` and `##ization` in a document came from one word.
- **The stem family is worth +3.21.** That is the largest single step here, 54.12 to 57.34, at 195 wins to 101 losses and p=0.0000. It is stemming done as query expansion: the query word fans into its family at search time and the best-matching member scores, so the stored pieces stay as they are.
- **The third channel adds +0.02 on top of the second.** All three together reach 57.36 against the family arm's 57.34, at 198 wins to 100 losses against the wordpiece baseline. Once the family is there, the exact word is nearly redundant with it.
- **The tail and the zero-rate move furthest.** p25 goes 21.84 to 29.61 with the family channel, roughly 2.4x the movement in the mean, and the share of queries returning nothing relevant falls 19.7% to 16.5%.

### Against the strongest baseline

A wordpiece index is the easy baseline to beat. The honest comparison is against a stemmed word index, which is what a production English analyzer actually gives you, and that is a much harder target. This table is the faithful architecture: word postings stored, piece lists derived at query time, nothing else materialised.

| arm                   | NDCG@10 | p50   | p25   | zero% | vs stemmed | W/L     | p      |
| --------------------- | ------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ---------- | ------- | ------ |
| word BM25, raw forms  | 54.33   | 58.56 | 22.01 | 20.2  | -1.48      | 110/135 | 0.1250 |
| word BM25, stemmed    | 55.81   | 61.31 | 25.91 | 17.6  | baseline   |         |        |
| piece + word          | 54.57   | 58.73 | 23.46 | 19.6  | -1.25      | 123/151 | 0.1027 |
| word + family         | 55.81   | 61.31 | 26.41 | 17.9  | -0.00      | 82/73   | 0.5206 |
| piece + word + family | 56.70   | 61.31 | 29.04 | 16.9  | +0.88      | 128/99  | 0.0629 |

Observations:

- **Pieces on their own do not reach a stemmer.** Adding the piece channel to a plain word index gives 54.57 against stemmed word BM25's 55.81, so it is 1.25 points behind at 123 wins to 151 losses, p=0.1027.
- **The full three-channel arm is +0.88 over the stemmed index.** 56.70 against 55.81, at 128 wins to 99 losses and p=0.0629. That misses the 0.05 bar, so it is promising and unproven, and a full-BEIR run is what would settle it.
- **Word plus family lands on the same mean as the stemmed index.** 55.81 both, while the tail differs: p25 26.41 against 25.91 and zero-rate 17.9% against 17.6%.
- **Paired against word plus family, the piece channel is worth +0.89.** 105 wins, 86 losses and 458 ties, p=0.1926. It touches only 191 of 649 queries and leaves 458 bit-identical, and among the 191 it moves, the median move is +0.72 and the mean +3.01. Ten queries go from zero to something, four go the other way.

### Where the derived channels lose

The 56.70 arm wins 128 queries against the stemmed index and loses 99, and the 99 have a shape:

| ------------ | ---- | ------ | -------- |
| FiQA2018     | 13   | 7      | +3.52    |

- **A short query resting on one rare word loses.** `von` in _what is the meaning of the name von_ is effectively the whole query. - **Losers carry more non-word terms.** Losses average 4.49 non-word terms against 2.56 on wins, so punctuation and numerals are being scored as content. Query length alone does not separate them: losses average 26.84 terms, wins 17.88, and ties 30.48.

## Positions, or a bound

Reading words back out of piece postings needs the trailing `##` check, and that check needs positions. A positional index stores one entry per occurrence instead of one per document, and the corpus holds 10,915,889 piece occurrences on top of 5,593,439 document entries, so a positional piece index is **3.26x** the word index.

You can skip the positions and take a counting bound instead. A word cannot occur more often than its rarest piece allows:

```
tf_word(d)  <=  min over pieces p of  tf_piece(p, d) / count_p(w)
```

It overshoots, and how far depends entirely on which words you ask about:

| population                        | bound       | true        | over by |
| --------------------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ------- |
| every word type in the vocabulary | 11,475,475  | 10,019,577  | 1.145x  |
| query terms, as actually queried  | 120,823,935 | 120,541,985 | 1.002x  |

Observations:

- **14.5% slack across the index, 0.2% across the words people search for.** Both numbers are correct and they measure different populations, so the one to quote depends on whether you are describing the index or the queries hitting it.
- **The second row counts a word once per query that contains it.** That is how retrieval actually reads the index, and query words skew common, so their pieces rarely leak into other words.
- **The slack piles up on the rare multi-piece tail.** Queries seldom touch it, and it is also where the retrieval cost of dropping positions shows up.

That is why the retrieval cost is small:

| arm                           | NDCG@10 | p50   | p25   | zero% | vs positional | W/L   | p      |
| ----------------------------- | ------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ------------- | ----- | ------ |
| word BM25, positional         | 54.33   | 58.56 | 22.01 | 20.2  |               |       |        |
| word BM25, position-free      | 54.17   | 59.58 | 21.40 | 19.7  | -0.16         | 37/72 | 0.0010 |
| piece + family, positional    | 56.64   | 61.31 | 29.04 | 16.9  |               |       |        |
| piece + family, position-free | 56.62   | 61.31 | 27.66 | 16.9  | -0.02         | 63/78 | 0.2383 |

Observations:

- **On its own the bound costs a measurable amount.** Word BM25 drops 0.16 points and loses 72 queries while winning 37, at p=0.0010. Small on the mean, consistent in sign, and it would show up on a bigger benchmark.
- **With a family channel present it costs 0.02 points.** 56.62 against 56.64, at 63 wins to 78 losses and p=0.2383. The family channel matches through a different route, so an inflated tf on one piece rarely reaches the top ten by itself.
- **The weak-query quartile is where it still shows.** p25 drops 29.04 to 27.66 while the mean and the median hold, and that is where the rare multi-piece words with loose bounds live.
- **The price of positions is 1.10x to 3.26x the word index.** That buys back 0.02 points once a stem family is in play, or 0.16 without one.

## What it costs per query

Deriving costs nothing on disk and real work on every query. Counting posting entries touched per query:

| system                          | postings per query | vs word BM25 |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------ |
| word BM25, scan query words     | 35,221             | 1.00x        |
| stemmed BM25, scan query stems  | 17,643             | 0.50x        |
| mine: family union over members | 18,106             | 0.51x        |
| mine: + derive pieces           | 54,365             | 1.54x        |

Observations:

- **The family union is cheap.** 18,106 postings against a materialised stem index's 17,643, so 0.51x of word BM25 and within 3% of the index it replaces. Unioning a word's family members at query time costs about what reading one merged stem posting list costs.
- **The piece derivation is the expensive half.** It alone adds 36,259 postings per query, another full word-BM25 scan, because a common piece sits inside a thousand different words and each of those is a separate posting list to open.
- **So the piece channel belongs in a rescore.** At 1.54x the postings for +0.89 paired, I would run word plus family first at 0.51x and derive pieces over the top few hundred candidates.
- **The auxiliary structure is small.** The map from a piece to the words containing it is 682,238 entries and holds no document ids at all, so it sits next to the vocabulary with the tokenizer.

## Stage 3: the model re-weights terms you already have

Everything so far assigns weights with a formula. A neural sparse model assigns them by learning, and it does the job better: on this benchmark SPLADE's own document weights score **63.36** where BM25 weights on the same documents score 57.09, both with the model running on the query side. The reason to care about the index layout is that the model emits weights over `bert-base-uncased` wordpieces, which is exactly what the postings are keyed by.

The first half of that move needs no reindex at all. Run the model on the query only, keep BM25 weights on the documents, and the query's term weights come from the model:

Observations:

- **The whole query-side move is worth +2.20 on the paired per-query mean.** 54.90 to 57.09, at 198 wins to 157 losses and p=0.0336, changing the ranking of 55% of the 649 queries. The document postings are the ones I built in stage 1, unchanged.
- **Re-weighting the query's own terms is the bigger half.** +1.33 comes from terms the query already contained, 54.90 to 56.23, and +0.86 from the expansion terms on top of that, 56.23 to 57.09.
- **Expansion is what reaches new documents.** The zero-rate goes 19.4% to 18.0% on re-weighting alone, then to 16.3% once the expansion terms are in, because re-weighting can only reorder the documents that already matched a query word.
- **Rescaling the expansions by one fitted scalar is worth another +0.53.** 57.09 to 57.62, and it trades zero-rate for mean: 17.3% against 16.3%, at the same p25 of 30.10.

The per-collection split behind that mean is wide:

| collection | vs pure BM25 |
| ---------- | ------------ |
| NQ         | +6.98        |
| SciFact    | +6.40        |
| FEVER      | +5.68        |
| DBPedia    | +4.26        |
| ArguAna    | -2.47        |
| Touche2020 | -3.94        |

Observations:

- **Two of the thirteen collections score better with pure BM25 and no model anywhere.** Touche2020 at -3.94 and ArguAna at -2.47, so switching the model on stays a per-collection decision even when it costs no reindex.
- **Four collections gain more than 4 points.** NQ +6.98, SciFact +6.40, FEVER +5.68 and DBPedia +4.26.
- **Both losers hold long queries.** One ArguAna query in this set runs 289 terms, and Touche2020 is an argument-retrieval collection. I have not traced a mechanism in those rows, so read it as a check to run on your own collection.

Turning the model on the document side as well is the step that does add postings, since an expansion term attaches to a document that never contained it. Here is what the document side is made of, with the model on the query side throughout:

| document-side weights                           | NDCG@10   |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| BM25                                            | 57.09     |
| the model's true literal weights, expansion cut | 58.67     |
| the model's full vector, expansion included     | 63.36     |
| `max(w_splade, 0.31*bm25)`                      | **64.45** |

Observations:

- **The document-side literal ceiling is +1.58.** The model's own weights on the words a document actually contains score 58.67 against BM25's 57.09, so better weighting of literal terms cannot buy more than that.
- **Half a SPLADE document vector is expansion.** 51.1% of its weight mass sits on terms the document never contained, which is why the literal ceiling is small and why the document side is the half that grows the index.
- **BM25 keeps its value under the model because it is independent of it.** I tried eleven ways to beat `max(w_splade, 0.31*bm25)` and none of them won, and a perfect literal-weight predictor used as the floor scores 63.36 against that rule's 64.45 at p=0.0039. The closer a floor gets to reproducing the model, the less it adds, and [the floor post](/blog/splade-bm25) has the full accounting.

## Key Takeaways

- **Index the wordpieces each document contains and you never migrate.** BM25 runs on those postings on day one, and a neural sparse model later re-weights the same terms in the same lists, because the model's vocabulary is the tokenizer's vocabulary.
- **The word and the stem family are derivable, so they cost no index.** All 217,815 piece posting lists rebuild exactly from word postings, and all 52,000 word types rebuild exactly from positional piece postings once a trailing `##` check removes 83,735 false postings, `un` alone gaining 1,565 of them on Touche2020. They cost query time instead: the family union runs at 0.51x the postings of word BM25 and deriving pieces at 1.54x.
- **The exact word channel is worth +1.29, the stem family +3.21.** Both over plain wordpiece BM25, at p=0.0001 and p=0.0000. Against a stemmed word index, the harder baseline, all three channels give +0.88 at 128 wins to 99 losses and p=0.0629, which is promising and unproven.
- **Dropping positions costs 0.02 points when a family channel is present.** 56.62 against 56.64 at p=0.2383, and 0.16 points without one at p=0.0010. Positions take the piece index from 1.10x to 3.26x the word index, so that is the price list.
- **The query-side half of the model needs no reindex at all.** It is worth +2.20 at 198 wins to 157 losses and p=0.0336, of which +1.33 is re-weighting terms the query already had. Two of thirteen collections still prefer pure BM25, Touche2020 at -3.94 and ArguAna at -2.47.
