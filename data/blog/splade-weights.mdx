[SPLADE](https://huggingface.co/naver/splade-v3) adds words to a document that the document never contained. Search for "retirement" and it can match a page that only ever says "401k". Every write-up covers that much.

What nobody shows is the _weights_ it gives those added words, and they have an odd shape. The top three or four are nearly tied, and then the list falls off a cliff. If you know [Zipf's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipf%27s_law) you would expect the opposite: a steep drop from first to second, then a long flat tail. SPLADE does the reverse.

I went looking for what the model had learned to produce that shape. It hasn't learned it. It comes out of two lines of arithmetic at the end of the network.

## The shape

Take one sentence and look at what a single word position votes for. Every position proposes weights over the whole 30,522-token vocabulary, and the document's final vector is the per-token max across positions, so we can pull one position out and read it on its own.

```
  "The bank raised interest rates because inflation kept climbing."
                                                        ↑
                                            this position votes:

   rank  word          weight     Zipf would say
     1   climbing       0.983         1.000
     2   rising         0.830         0.500      ← still 84% of the top
     3   climb          0.799         0.333      ← still 81%
     4   rise           0.544         0.250
     5   increasing     0.495         0.200
     6   trend          0.125         0.167      ← cliff
     7   increase       0.073         0.143
     8   spike          0.065         0.125
     9   everything else  0.000
```

Rank 2 keeps 84% of rank 1's weight where Zipf predicts 50%. Ranks 6 onward collapse to nothing where Zipf predicts a slow taper. The whole list is 8 words out of 30,522.

Across 1,297 content-word positions, `w₂/w₁` has a median of **0.743**. Zipf says 0.500 always, so this is not a quirk of one sentence.

## Where it comes from

Before the last two operations the numbers are boringly regular. Fit the raw logits against log rank over the top 200 vocabulary entries and we get this:

| position kind     | slope | R²        | positions |
| ----------------- | ----- | --------- | --------- |
| content           | 0.505 | 0.923     | 2,289     |
| subword           | 0.539 | 0.887     | 220       |
| stopword          | 0.540 | **0.986** | 1,885     |
| punctuation       | 0.605 | 0.979     | 917       |
| `[CLS]` / `[SEP]` | 0.534 | 0.972     | 120       |

A straight line, and a tight one. In every case the slope is about **0.5**, which means

$$
\text{logit}(r) \approx C - 0.5\ln r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(r) \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}
$$

Zipf's law for word frequency is `1/r`. The MLM head's vocabulary distribution is `1/√r`, so **half Zipf's exponent**. Flatter, and no plateau or cliff anywhere in it.

Then the last two operations get hold of it.

### The relu makes the cliff

SPLADE's weight is `log(1 + relu(logit))`. The relu sets every negative logit to zero. On the `climbing` position, **8 of the top 200 logits are above zero**. Everything from rank 9 down isn't small, it's gone.

That's the cliff. It isn't a steep decline, it's a hard edge, and its position is wherever the logits happen to cross zero.

### The log1p makes the plateau

`log(1+x)` compresses large values much harder than small ones. Watch the top of the list:

```
  rank    logit    log(1+relu)     gap to rank 1
    1     1.673       0.983
    2     1.293       0.830        29% in logits  ->  16% in weights
    3     1.223       0.799        37%            ->  19%
```

A 29% gap in logit space arrives as a 16% gap in weight space. The compression squashes the head of the list flat, and that's the plateau.

- **The median logit crosses zero at rank 7.** And rank 7 is exactly where content positions start running out of terms, 1,297 of them at rank 6 and 1,131 at rank 7. The cliff is not a decline, it is the relu deleting everything on the wrong side of that line.

Put the two charts side by side and the whole mechanism is there. The logits are a clean decline that crosses zero around rank 7. `relu` deletes the negative half, which makes the cliff. `log1p` squashes the positive half, which turns a 2x-per-rank decline into the near-flat plateau of 1.00, 0.77, 0.55.

## Fitting it properly

Six decay families against the real weights, median absolute error across all positions:

| position kind | exponential | power law | Mandelbrot | stretched | linear | log-linear |
| ------------- | ----------- | --------- | ---------- | --------- | ------ | ---------- |
| content       | 20.8%       | 32.9%     | 20.5%      | **11.4%** | 24.6%  | 15.5%      |
| subword       | 20.6%       | 30.9%     | 20.2%      | **12.0%** | 26.6%  | 15.8%      |
| stopword      | 21.0%       | 34.2%     | 23.3%      | **11.5%** | 17.9%  | 12.3%      |
| punctuation   | 21.0%       | 33.6%     | 22.1%      | **11.4%** | 18.8%  | 13.8%      |

Observations:

- **The power law is the worst fit of the six**, at 33%. This is the one people reach for when they hear "vocabulary distribution", and it fails because it has the shape backwards: steep head, fat tail, where the data has a flat head and no tail.
- **A stretched exponential halves the error**, from 20.8% to 11.4%. It is `w = A·exp(-k·r^β)` with one extra knob over a plain exponential, and the fitted `β` is **1.65** for content positions. `β = 1` is a plain exponential and `β > 1` is a compressed one, which is exactly "flat head, then a sharper cliff".
- **How much of that is the `log1p`?** Refit in raw `relu(logit)` space and `β` drops from 1.65 to **1.25**. So of the 0.65 excess above a plain exponential, removing the log accounts for 0.40, about 60%. The remaining 40% is genuine flatness in the logits.
- **Log-linear wins on `[CLS]`/`[SEP]`**, 10.7% against the stretched fit's 11.8%. Those positions have the cleanest power-law logits of any kind (R² 0.986), so the power law survives the relu there and nowhere else.

## Why it matters

A power law has a fat tail, so truncating it throws away real mass. A compressed exponential doesn't. That distinction is what decides whether we can prune the index, and it is why I went looking at the shape in the first place.

And you can, aggressively. Dropping every expansion term below a single global weight threshold of 0.5 removes **46% of the postings** and costs 0.0001 NDCG@10 across 13 [NanoBEIR](https://huggingface.co/collections/zeta-alpha-ai/nanobeir-66e1a0af21dfd93e620cd9f6) datasets (0.6250 against 0.6251, bootstrap CI [−0.0047, +0.0043]). One constant, applied per posting, no per-document sorting.

That works because of the cliff. The terms below the threshold were already near-zero, and there are no surprises further down, because the relu already deleted everything that would have been there.

## Key Takeaways

- One SPLADE position gives its top few added words nearly equal weight and then falls to zero, which is the reverse of the Zipf shape people expect. Rank 2 keeps 74% of rank 1's weight at the median, where Zipf predicts 50%.
- The shape isn't learned. The raw logits are a straight line against log rank with slope about 0.5, so `p(r) ~ 1/√r`, half Zipf's exponent, with no plateau or cliff in it.
- The relu makes the cliff by deleting every negative logit, leaving 8 surviving words out of 30,522 on the position I traced. The `log1p` makes the plateau by compressing the top of the list, and accounts for about 60% of the departure from a plain exponential.
- A stretched exponential with `β = 1.65` halves the fit error against a plain exponential. A power law is the worst of six families tested, at 33% median error.
- Because the tail is thin rather than fat, a single global threshold of 0.5 prunes 46% of the index for 0.0001 NDCG@10.
